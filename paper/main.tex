\begin{abstract}
% Fuente: docs/sweep-curva-multiescala-2026-07-10.md,
% docs/sweep-planner-ab-2026-07-11.md, docs/sweep-lead-3brazos-2026-07-10.md
Agentic coding harnesses are designed for frontier models; whether ---
and which --- scaffolding transfers to small, local models (1--8B) is
largely unmeasured. We present \braze{}, an open-source harness built
to make that question measurable: every reliability lever is
independently switchable per benchmark row, every run is reproducible
from a commit hash and suite fingerprint, and design decisions were
pre-registered before their evaluations ran. Across 1{,}520 runs
spanning four executor scales and four harness configurations, a
locally-served lead model that proactively opens each turn pins the
composite system
at the lead's \emph{own} ceiling regardless of executor scale: a 1B
executor rises from 19\% to 89\% pass rate, outperforming unassisted
3B (68\%) and 7B (80\%) baselines (scale and model family are not
fully separated here: the 1B is Llama, the larger executors Qwen).
Pre-registered controls temper that headline: the lead alone reaches
91\% on the same suite ($n{=}285$), statistically indistinguishable
from the composite (no equivalence margin was pre-declared), and a
from-scratch lead+executor loop with none of \braze{}'s other levers
lands in the same band --- the gain is routing to a capable model, not
emergent capability of the composition or of the harness's
engineering. A context lever (a prose plan injected into the prompt)
\emph{harms} at both extremes of scale through a single degeneration
mode (turns yielding no usable output); a pre-registered iteration
localizes the failure to the plan's \emph{delivery} format and repairs
the harm at both scales --- though no delivery clears the
pre-registered adoption bar (the largest movement, +12\,pp on a 3B
executor via a typed task list, has an interval marginally crossing
zero). Inspecting the served chat templates locates that failure
outside the models entirely: a plan injected in the assistant role is
rendered as a turn to continue rather than a question to answer, which
is why the damage is scale-independent and why changing the role
repairs it. We further isolate the lead's
mechanism experimentally (proactive opening, not reactive escalation)
and show that two-level tool deferral cuts prompt tokens $5$--$6\times$
at a real, scale-dependent correctness cost whose diagnosed mechanism
is compositional brittleness of the small model, not tool findability.
A pre-registered external anchor --- 60 tasks sampled
deterministically from the Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard,
graded by an argument-level AST checker committed before the sweep ran
--- replicates the central result outside our own tasks: the lead
lever moves the 1B executor by $+72$\,pp on the anchor versus
$+70.5$\,pp on the authored suite, with baseline ordering preserved
and the ceiling-pinning null intact. A second pre-registered anchor
--- 20 SWE-bench Lite instances graded by the official test-based
harness --- bounds the same claims at repository scale: no lever
turns the unassisted 1B (zero patches in 40 runs) into a
repository-capable agent, but the lead lever's behavioral effect
transfers with statistical support (patches 0/40~$\to$~6/40,
$p{=}0.026$), its resolution effect directionally (0~$\to$~2/40,
including one Django issue resolved in both repetitions), and the
ceiling-pinning pattern holds (composite 2/40 vs.\ lead alone 3/40).
For practitioners the takeaway is that not all scaffolding helps by
default: which lever helps, and in which delivery format, is itself
scale-dependent and must be measured per deployment rather than
assumed from what works at the frontier.
\keywords{agentic software engineering \and LLM agents \and small
language models \and tool calling \and benchmark \and ablation study \and
pre-registration}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% Fuente: CLAUDE.md (tesis), PLAN.md, docs/SOTA-2026-07.md

Coding agents --- Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Goose --- are, in their
default configuration, built around and evaluated against frontier
models: tens to hundreds of billions of parameters, served over an API.
The scaffolding around the model --- how tools are presented, how
context is managed across turns, how a stalled turn is recovered ---
is designed and tuned against that regime. At the same time, cost,
data-privacy, and latency constraints push a growing share of
deployments toward small, local models in the 1--8B range: models cheap
enough to run on commodity hardware, private enough to keep code
on-premises, and fast enough for an interactive loop without a network
round trip. Whether the scaffolding built for frontier models continues
to help at that scale, which of its components still pull their weight,
and which start actively hurting once the model itself is the
bottleneck rather than the interface, is a question none of the
production harnesses above answer: their design choices are not
published as independently switchable, measured levers, and their
target model class is a moving frontier, not the small end of it.

Our thesis is that the harness is a variable that can compensate for
model scale, not merely a wrapper around it. The direct antecedent is
SWE-agent's Agent-Computer Interface work \citep{yang2024sweagent},
which showed --- via a controlled ablation on GPT-4 Turbo --- that the
interface between an agent and its computer moves SWE-bench Lite
\citep{jimenez2024swebench} resolve rate by amounts comparable to
switching the underlying model:
removing the edit command's linting guardrail alone costs 3.0 points,
removing the constrained file viewer costs up to 5.3, and an
unconstrained iterative search command costs more than not offering
search at all (Table~\ref{tab:aci}, reproduced from their Table~3). That
result was demonstrated at one operating point, a single frontier
model. We ask the question orthogonally: across a range of small,
locally-deployable executor scales, does each harness lever still help,
by how much, and does the answer change sign as the model gets smaller
or larger. Small models are exactly where a harness's design choices are
least tested and most consequential --- prior work on small-model
tool-calling (\S\ref{sec:related}) documents brittleness to prompt
format, in-context example count, and reasoning length that a frontier
model simply absorbs.

Although our benchmark tasks are deliberately small, the question they
instrument is a software-engineering one. Coding agents are entering
the everyday development toolchain, and a harness's design decisions
--- how tools are surfaced to the model, how context is compacted
across turns, when a stronger model intervenes --- are software design
decisions whose consequences are today argued from intuition rather
than measured: exactly the gap between practice and evidence that
empirical software engineering exists to close. The suite's five
skills are the primitives agentic coding workflows compose ---
locating and reading the right file, editing it, chaining dependent
steps, recovering from a failed action, and rejecting
plausible-but-wrong alternatives --- chosen to isolate failure modes
rather than to reproduce end-to-end tasks like issue resolution
(\S\ref{sec:threats} discusses what this choice does and does not
license). The harness and bench are themselves infrastructure for
empirical study --- every design lever doubles as an ablation flag ---
in the tradition of benchmark-and-tooling contributions to empirical
software engineering research.

Answering that question rigorously requires more than one more
benchmark suite: it requires a harness whose reliability levers are
independently switchable per benchmark row, so that a claim about lever
$X$ at scale $Y$ is an ablation and not a confound with every other
design choice; a bench whose runs are reproducible from a commit hash
and a suite fingerprint, so that a result can be re-run rather than
merely re-read; and, because several of the levers we evaluate have
plausible arguments on both sides before the data comes in, a discipline
of writing down the adoption criterion \emph{before} running the sweep
that decides it. We built \braze{}, an open-source agentic harness, to
make this infrastructure the artifact rather than a one-off script
around the actual contribution.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The harness-vs-scale curve} (Fig.~\ref{fig:curva}):
    4 harness configurations $\times$ 4 executor scales, $n{=}95$ per
    cell, one binary. A pre-registered solo baseline of the lead model
    shows the capability lever pins the composite at the lead's own
    unassisted ceiling at every scale we tested --- so the lever's
    apparent gain (+70\,pp at 1B $\to$ $-4$\,pp at the ceiling) decays
    as the corollary of a rising baseline under a roughly constant
    composite, not as capability transfer between the models; the
    context lever harms at both extremes via one degeneration mode
    (\S\ref{sec:curve}).
  \item \textbf{A first external comparison point}: a lead+executor
    loop implemented from scratch, with none of \braze{}'s other
    levers, lands within noise of both \braze{}'s full 1B+lead
    composite and \texttt{gemma4:e4b} alone --- three independent
    measurements in one 87--91\% band, replicated at $n{=}285$ per arm
    (pooled across two sweeps each) with the null holding at tighter
    confidence intervals, suggesting the
    headline gain at 1B is attributable to the lead/executor
    composition pattern and the helper model's own capability, not to
    \braze{}'s additional engineering specifically, at least at a scale
    this sample size can detect (\S\ref{sec:external}).
  \item \textbf{An external task anchor}: a pre-registered 60-task
    subset of the Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard
    \citep{bfcl} --- sampling seed, argument-level AST grader, and
    readings all committed before the sweep ran --- replicates the
    central lever outside the authored suite ($+72.0$\,pp at 1B
    versus $+70.5$\,pp authored, both far from zero; three single-turn
    categories), preserves the
    baseline ordering, reproduces the ceiling-pinning null, and
    quantifies the thesis in one number per model: the gap between
    naming the right tool and producing correct arguments is
    $+47$\,pp at 1B but $3$--$4$\,pp for competent models
    (\S\ref{sec:bfcl}).
  \item \textbf{A repository-level anchor}: a pre-registered 20-instance
    SWE-bench Lite slice graded by the official test-based harness
    bounds the claims at repository scale --- the unassisted 1B never
    even edits (0 patches / 40 runs), the lead lever's behavioral
    effect transfers outside noise (patches 0/40~$\to$~6/40,
    $p{=}0.026$) with resolution moving directionally (one Django
    issue resolved in both repetitions), and the pinned-ceiling
    pattern replicates on execution-graded tasks
    (\S\ref{sec:swebench}).
  \item \textbf{Causal isolation of the dominant lever}: a three-arm
    experiment separating proactive opening from reactive escalation
    shows the entire gain comes from the former; the reactive trigger
    is structurally blind to the semantic failures that dominate
    (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}).
  \item \textbf{A negative result diagnosed and repaired under
    pre-registration} (Fig.~\ref{fig:rescate}): the planner's collapse
    (both extremes at 49\%) was a delivery bug, not a property of
    planning --- and the served chat templates locate the bug
    precisely, in the prefill convention that turns an assistant-role
    injection into a continuation rather than a question. The
    pre-registered iteration eliminates the harm at both
    scales, yet the committed criterion, applied literally, still
    declines to adopt any delivery (the largest movement, +12\,pp at
    3B via a typed task list, marginally crosses zero) --- a verdict
    we report with a dated deviation note (\S\ref{sec:planner}).
  \item \textbf{Cost-side levers quantified, honestly}: two-level tool
    deferral ($5$--$6\times$ fewer prompt tokens at a $-13$/$-24$\,pp
    correctness cost, whose diagnosed mechanism is compositional
    brittleness rather than findability or distraction,
    Fig.~\ref{fig:searchtools}); a pre-registered investigation of
    decoder-level constrained decoding as an alternative to harness-side
    textual rescue, whose single permitted iteration --- tightening the
    forced schema from generic to per-tool --- verifies the mechanism
    cleanly (schema failures $99\to0$) while making pass rate
    \emph{worse}, closing the comparison decisively in the harness's
    favor (\S\ref{sec:constrained});
  \item \textbf{\braze{} itself}: an open-source Rust harness where
    every lever is a per-row benchmark ablation
    (\texttt{+ablate:\{no-rescue, no-prune, no-lead, task-list, ...\}}),
    released with the full sweep data for every result reported here
    (\S``Data availability'').
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% Fuente: docs/SOTA-2026-07.md; docs/opencode-a-braze.md;
% docs/harness-engineering-hooks-skills-2026-07-10.md

\paragraph{Agent-computer interfaces.} SWE-agent's ACI work
\citep{yang2024sweagent} is the founding evidence that the interface
between an agent and its environment is a first-class performance
variable, not implementation detail (Table~\ref{tab:aci},
\S\ref{sec:intro}). Among its ablations, collapsing old tool
observations to a single line while keeping the most recent ones in
full costs 3.0 points relative to sending the full, uncollapsed history
--- the direct antecedent of \braze{}'s own tactical observation
collapse (\S\ref{sec:harness}). Their study holds the model fixed (GPT-4
Turbo) and varies the interface; we hold the interface's \emph{levers}
fixed as a menu and vary the model's scale, asking whether the same
levers that helped a frontier model still help, by how much, and in
which direction, once the model itself becomes the bottleneck.

\paragraph{Small language models as agents.} Three findings from the
small-model literature motivate specific design choices in \braze{}
rather than being merely background. The Qwen3-Coder-Next technical
report \citep{qwen3coder} documents that transfer between agent
scaffolds is limited: models trained on one scaffold's tool-call
trajectories do not transfer cleanly to another, and, on the report's
own template-following benchmark across five IDE/CLI scaffolds, one
frontier model's format-following accuracy swings from 84.0 to 14.0
depending on which scaffold serves it (Table 2 of that report) ---
evidence that a model's tool-call template is a trained-in property a
harness cannot assume away, not a report that this effect scales with
model size specifically. This motivates \braze{}'s per-model-family
textual rescue (\S\ref{sec:harness}): recovering a tool call the model
emitted in its own native template --- tagged, bare-JSON, or pythonic
--- rather than penalizing a syntactically well-formed but
differently-shaped response. Lu et al.'s survey of small language
models
\citep{lu2024smalllm} reports that in-context learning is weak and
non-monotonic at small scale: five-shot prompting adds only 2.1 points
on average across their 70-model survey, and some models degrade with
more shots rather than improving. This is an argument against padding
the executor's prompt with worked tool-call examples, and in favor of
\braze{}'s alternative --- an explicit, structured plan or task list
(\S\ref{sec:planner}) --- as the vehicle for guidance instead. Qi's
finding that longer chain-of-thought budgets are non-monotonic for
function-calling accuracy \citep{qi2026briefbetter}, with brief
reasoning acting as a "function-routing" step that overzealous
elaboration erodes, is the literature counterpart of a failure mode we
observe directly in \S\ref{sec:planner}: a prose plan rendered as the
executor's own text degenerates into empty responses at both ends of
our scale range, which we read as the executor over-committing to
narrating rather than acting.

\paragraph{Multi-model orchestration.} Two production systems pair a
stronger and a weaker model within a single agentic loop, and both
inform our own lead/executor design (\S\ref{sec:harness}) without fully
anticipating it. Goose's \texttt{GOOSE\_LEAD\_MODEL} \citep{goose} is
purely \emph{reactive}: a lead model opens the first few turns by
default, then hands off to the worker, and only re-escalates back to the
lead when a failure detector counts consecutive tool-call failures. No
quantitative evaluation of this mechanism is published. Aider's
architect/editor split \citep{aider} is closer to a controlled result:
routing a strong model's plan through a --- possibly identical, possibly
cheaper --- editor model for the mechanical edit gains 3--4 points over
a single-pass strong model doing both, which the Aider team attributes
to freeing the planning role from edit-format constraints rather than to
using two models per se. Our three-arm mechanism isolation
(\S\ref{sec:mechanism}) is, to our knowledge, the first controlled
comparison between the \emph{proactive}-opening and \emph{reactive}-escalation
variants of this pattern in the same harness: we find the entire
measured gain attributable to proactive opening, with the reactive
trigger structurally blind to the semantic failure modes that dominate
our suite. Separately, Corradini et al.'s systematic review of small
language models \citep{corradini2025} reports test-time voting across
repeated attempts as a cheap capability lever at small scale --- the
most striking such data point being a 1B-parameter model that overtakes
a 405B model on math problems under compute-optimal test-time scaling
\citep{liu2025tts}. \braze{}'s
best-of-$n$ voting over independently generated tool-call candidates
(Table~\ref{tab:levers}) applies the same test-time-scaling idea to the
tool-calling decision itself.

\paragraph{Agentic harnesses.} Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, and Gemini
CLI embody design principles --- deferred tool loading so only names
(not full schemas) occupy the prompt by default, differential context
compaction that separates durable state from a tactical window, and
layered permission models that auto-approve low-risk actions while
gating higher-risk ones --- that inform \braze{}'s own architecture
(\S\ref{sec:harness}) but that none of these systems publishes as
independently measured, ablatable results: the principles are
documented, not benchmarked. The OpenHands Software Agent SDK
\citep{wang2026openhands} is closer to an exception: its append-only
event stream with resume-by-replay is reported to substantially reduce
system-attributable failures with negligible event-sourcing overhead,
which we read as external validation of \braze{}'s own event-log design
(\S\ref{sec:harness}) --- though, again, not decomposed into
independently switchable levers the way our ablation suffixes are.
The pattern held as this paper was being finalized: Kimi Code
\citep{kimicode}, released by a fourth major lab in July 2026, ships
built-in \texttt{plan}/\texttt{explore}/\texttt{coder} subagents in
isolated contexts, lifecycle hooks that gate risky tool calls, and a
skills marketplace --- documented as features, with no published
measurement of what any of them contributes.
\braze{}'s contribution relative to all of the above is not a new
harness principle but making an existing menu of principles
\emph{switchable and measured}: every lever in Table~\ref{tab:levers}
ships as a per-row benchmark ablation
(\texttt{+ablate:\{no-rescue,\allowbreak no-prune,\allowbreak
no-lead,\allowbreak task-list,\allowbreak\ldots\}}), so a claim like
"the lead model helps" is a number with a confidence interval and a
commit hash behind it, not a design note.

\paragraph{A pedagogy that arrived before its evidence.} While this
paper was being finalized the design space it measures acquired not
only a name but a curriculum. Walking Labs' open-source \emph{Learn
Harness Engineering} \citep{walkinglabs2026} defines the discipline as
"building a complete working environment around the model so it
produces reliable results \ldots\ not about writing better prompts",
decomposes it into five subsystems --- instructions, state,
verification, scope, session lifecycle --- and ships thirteen lectures,
seven projects and copy-ready templates. Its vocabulary is better than
ours and we adopt parts of it in \S\ref{sec:discussion}; two of its
five subsystems, scope and session lifecycle, have no counterpart in
\braze{}, which bounds what we can say about the taxonomy as a whole.

The relevant point for this paper is evidential rather than
terminological. The course does cite evidence, and more of it than a
glance suggests --- SWE-bench Verified pass rates with an explicit
caveat that its tasks are selected, a five-month Codex-only
million-line engineering report, and a case study on a 15{,}000-line
application --- but none of it is an ablation, and its most-cited
result is confounded in a way that matters here. The comparison it
leads with holds model and prompt fixed and reports a bare run of
twenty minutes and \$9 producing something nonfunctional against a
harnessed run of six hours and \$200 producing a working artefact. The
two conditions differ by roughly $22\times$ in spend as well as in
harness, so the outcome cannot be attributed to the harness: the design
does not separate the lever from the budget. This is the same gap as
the vendor documentation above, one rung further along --- the
principles are now taught rather than merely documented, and still not
decomposed into independently switchable levers with confidence
intervals. That is the gap this paper's ablation suffixes exist to
close, and it is why we read a curriculum's arrival as raising the
value of measurement rather than substituting for it. (We verified
these characterizations against the course's first lecture and its
published structure, not against all thirteen.)

Against the curriculum sits a practitioner objection with the opposite
bound. Dex's \emph{Why Software Factories Fail} \citep{dex2026wsff}
argues that "no amount of harness engineering or loopsmaxxing can solve
what is fundamentally a model-training issue", supported by industry
telemetry the author explicitly declines to treat as causal ("more of a
correlation signal than a verifiable smoking gun"). Our results speak
to both positions and endorse neither wholesale. Several levers in
Table~\ref{tab:levers} measure conditional or scale-dependent rather
than the reliable wins a curriculum implies, which supports the
objection; the trajectory-length instability we report is a
round-budget effect that does not generalize across models, where the
course states a wall-clock threshold. But the failure mode the
objection attributes to training --- degradation that no gate reports
--- is a detection problem, and detection is precisely what a harness
can be built to do. \S\ref{sec:discussion} returns to this with a case
in which neither position is quite right: a failure the harness could
not repair and could nonetheless make legible and cheap.

\paragraph{Breadth versus depth.} This paper sits inside a fast-growing
empirical tradition of evaluating LLMs on software-engineering tasks
under controlled designs --- recent examples in this journal alone
measure refactoring consistency \citep{zhang2026refactoring} and
code-generation robustness \citep{liu2026robustness} --- and treats
its benchmark-and-harness pair as research infrastructure in the sense
of benchmarking-as-empirical-standard
\citep{hasselbring2021benchmarking}: the instrument is versioned,
fingerprinted, and shipped so the measurement can be re-run, not just
read. A complementary tradition
studies agent frameworks by mining many of them at once. Hasan et al.'s
study of testing practices across open-source AI agent frameworks and
agentic applications \citep{hasan2026testing} is this paper's closest
peer along that axis: where we hold one harness's lever menu fixed and
vary model scale under a pre-registered ablation design, that line of
work samples broadly across independently built frameworks to
characterize practices as they exist in the wild, trading the causal
leverage of an ablation (this lever, this scale, this confidence
interval) for coverage of how the ecosystem actually builds and tests
these systems outside any single research harness. Neither approach
subsumes the other: a corpus study can report what fraction of real
agent frameworks test tool-call recovery at all, a question this
paper's single-harness design cannot answer; this paper can report
that a specific recovery lever moves pass rate by a specific, confidence-
bounded amount at a specific scale, a question a corpus study surveying
practice rather than intervening on it cannot answer either. We see the
two as complementary evidence a future synthesis should combine rather
than as competing methodologies, and note explicitly that the breadth
this paper deliberately does not attempt is exactly what that line of
work provides.

\section{The \braze{} Harness}\label{sec:harness}
% Fuente: PLAN.md (arquitectura), CLAUDE.md (palancas)

\braze{} is a Rust workspace of 14 crates built as an agentic
coding-CLI harness in the style of Claude Code and Codex, but designed
from the outset for the experiment this paper reports rather than as a
product. Three traits form a frozen contract that every other design
decision is built against and never bypasses: \texttt{ToolProvider}
(local built-in tools and MCP servers implement the same
\texttt{list\_stubs}/\texttt{resolve\_schema}/\texttt{invoke} interface,
so a tool's origin is invisible to the engine), \texttt{ModelBackend}
(Anthropic, Ollama, and OpenRouter implement the same streaming
completion interface, so a benchmark row's provider is a configuration
value, not a code path), and \texttt{SessionStore} paired with a
\texttt{ContextCompactor} (every turn is an append-only event log; the
compactor decides what a model sees on replay without ever mutating the
log itself). Everything else --- how tools are advertised, how failed
tool calls are recovered, whether a second model opens a turn, how
context is trimmed under budget --- is a lever behind one of these
traits, switchable independently and by default matching what a real
\texttt{braze} invocation would run. Table~\ref{tab:levers} lists the
levers this paper evaluates, their mechanism, the \texttt{+ablate:}
suffix \texttt{braze-bench} accepts to toggle them for one benchmark
row, their production default, and where in this paper each is
measured; several of the mechanically simplest levers (post-edit
compiler feedback, best-of-$n$ voting, harness notes, the unablated
observation collapse) are implemented and shipped but not isolated by
a dedicated ablation sweep in this paper, which we mark honestly rather
than report as measured.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
% Fuente: braze-bench (diseño), docs de sweeps (metadata)

Every run in this paper is produced by \texttt{braze-bench}, a harness
comparison CLI shipped in the same repository: it drives one or more
\texttt{ModelBackend}/lever configurations (``arms'') against a shared
task suite inside a per-task sandbox, and reports per-arm pass rate,
round count, latency, and token/cost estimates. Our suite
(\texttt{crates/braze-bench/suites/default.toml}) has 19 tasks spanning
five skills: \texttt{single\_tool} (7 tasks --- read/grep/glob/write/
edit/shell, one call each), \texttt{no\_tool} (3 --- a question the
model should answer directly, with no tool call expected), \texttt{
multi\_step} (3 --- a short chain of dependent tool calls), \texttt{
error\_recovery} (3 --- a first call fails on a wrong filename or
extension and the model must recover), and \texttt{distractor\_selection}
(3 --- multiple plausible files are present and only one is correct).
Each task runs in a freshly seeded sandbox directory populated with its
own fixture files, under a 180-second per-attempt timeout, with sampling
temperature fixed at 0.2 uniformly across every backend and provider in
a sweep (Ollama, Anthropic, and OpenRouter alike) so that a comparison
across providers is a comparison of models, not of sampling regimes.
Every (task, arm) cell in the results below is 5 repetitions ($n{=}95$
per arm across the full suite; $n{=}15$ when a table reports one of the
3-task skills, $n{=}35$ for \texttt{single\_tool}, which has 7 tasks),
aggregated with 95\% Wilson confidence intervals --- following standard
guidance on repeated runs and interval reporting for randomized
algorithms in software engineering
\citep{wohlin2012experimentation,arcuri2011practical}.

\paragraph{Executors and fixed roles.} The executor scale range spans
llama3.2:1b (a generic instruction-tuned model with native tool-calling
support but no function-calling specialization), qwen2.5:3b and
qwen2.5:7b (function-calling-tuned), and qwen3.5-coder (the strongest
locally-served model available to us, used as the scale ceiling rather
than a frontier anchor). Where a lead or planner role is composed with
an executor, gemma4:e4b serves that role uniformly across every arm
that uses one, so that a scale-curve result is attributable to the
\emph{executor's} scale and not to a confounded choice of helper model
per row. Table~\ref{tab:models} lists every model with its parameter
count, quantization as served, and role. Two of its rows matter for
interpreting the curve honestly: the executor axis mixes family,
tuning, and one quantization level (the 1B is Q8\_0, the rest
Q4\_K\_M), so ``scale'' should be read as a deployment-tier ladder
rather than a controlled parameter sweep (\S\ref{sec:threats}); and
the lead model is not small relative to its executors --- 8.0B total
parameters ($\approx$4B active per token, MoE), comparable in total
size to the 7B executor. All Ollama-served arms explicitly request
\texttt{options.num\_ctx}${}={}$8192 (the harness default,
deliberately above Ollama's 2048--4096 Modelfile defaults, whose
silent front-truncation an agentic prompt exceeds within a few
rounds); the engine additionally enforces its own context budget below
that limit via compaction, so the full-inventory prompts of
\S\ref{sec:searchtools} ($\sim$7.5K tokens per request for the
206-tool catalog) fit the served window rather than being silently
truncated --- the 1{,}500-tool extrapolation there is precisely the
point where they no longer would.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Every sweep JSON embeds the harness git
commit, a suite fingerprint, and per-model digests. One component of
the stack was \emph{not} recorded in sweep metadata at collection
time: the version of the Ollama server itself --- a genuine gap, since
chat-template rendering, the layer \S\ref{sec:planner} locates a
mechanism in, changes across Ollama releases. We close it two ways.
Retrospectively: the inference node's service journal and server
binary (installed 2026-06-08, predating every sweep in
Table~\ref{tab:sweeps}) show a single version, Ollama 0.30.7, serving
the entire collection window --- no sweep in this paper spans a server
upgrade. Prospectively: the harness now embeds the server's reported
version (\texttt{/api/version}) in every sweep's metadata, so this
identity can never again go unrecorded. Confidence
intervals are 95\% Wilson ($n{=}95$ per cell unless noted), with
Newcombe/MOVER intervals for differences between arms --- Wilson score
bounds \emph{without} continuity correction, applied uniformly (where
an earlier analysis document computed an interval with continuity
correction, this paper re-reports the uncorrected value and flags any
comparison whose reading is sensitive to that choice). Two disciplines
govern comparisons. First, levels measured in different sweeps are
never compared directly; a delta is only computed between arms
collected under the same conditions --- either arms of one multi-arm
sweep, or single-arm sweeps run as a same-day batch on the same commit,
node, and sampling configuration (the solo-baseline and power sweeps of
\S\ref{sec:curve}--\ref{sec:external} are the latter case; where an arm
pools a slice from an older commit, we report a slice-homogeneity check
and a same-commit sensitivity estimate alongside the pooled number).
Second, because $n{=}95$ is 19 tasks $\times$ 5 repetitions rather than
95 independent draws, runs cluster by task; Wilson intervals quantify
sampling noise for the estimand ``pass rate on this fixed suite,''
while generalization to a broader task population is wider. For the
headline claims we therefore also report task-level cluster bootstrap
intervals (19 tasks resampled with replacement, $B{=}20{,}000$, tasks
resampled jointly across the arms of a delta), and we flag which
conclusions survive them. Throughout, ``statistically
indistinguishable'' means the 95\% interval on the within-comparison
delta crosses zero; no equivalence margin was pre-declared, so such
statements are failures to detect an effect at the stated interval
width, not demonstrations of equivalence --- the formal instrument for
the latter would be an equivalence test against a justified margin
\citep{lakens2017equivalence}, which we did not pre-commit and
therefore do not retrofit. Finally, this paper reports
on the order of twenty between-arm interval comparisons in total and
applies no family-wise correction across them
\citep[cf.][]{demsar2006statistical}: adopt/reject decisions
are governed by the pre-registered criteria below, but any individual
uncorrected interval should be read with that multiplicity in mind
(flagged again as a conclusion-validity threat in
\S\ref{sec:threats}).

\paragraph{Grader validation.} Pass/fail is decided by scripted
assertions (tool-call identity, substring checks on the final response,
substring checks on written files), and \S\ref{sec:threats} already
discloses one historical assertion bug (an earlier text-match check
that accepted a model's pre-tool-call narration as satisfying a content
check). To validate the current grader independently of that
disclosure, we added opt-in transcript preservation to
\texttt{braze-bench} (\texttt{BRAZE\_BENCH\_KEEP\_SESSIONS}, previously
an uncommitted local patch) and hand-graded 62 preserved runs --- the
two arms most central to \S\ref{sec:curve}'s headline number
(\texttt{llama3.2:1b} baseline and \texttt{+lead}, 19 tasks each) and
the two arms most central to \S\ref{sec:searchtools}'s (\texttt{
qwen2.5:3b} deferred and full-inventory, 6 tasks $\times$ 2 repetitions
each) --- against the automated verdict. Agreement was 62/62 (100\%);
one run's transcript additionally shows the assertion design working as
intended rather than merely agreeing by luck: a \texttt{
noisy\_multi\_step} run wrote the literal string \texttt{"int\_a +
int\_b"} to its output file while its final response text claimed "the
sum...is 30", a confabulation a text-only check would have missed
(the substring "30" appears in that sentence) but the paired
file-content check catches. Full methodology and the two additional
qualitative findings (strict tool-identity assertions failing a
task solved via a different-but-effective tool; the historical bug's
absence from this sample) are in
\texttt{docs/grader-validation-2026-07-13.md}. The hand-grading was
performed by the author; there is no independent second rater, a
limitation the 62/62 agreement figure inherits.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} Two of this paper's results were governed
by an adoption/rejection criterion written down, and committed to the
repository, before the sweep that decided it ran: the planner's
delivery-format iteration (\S\ref{sec:planner}) and the tool-deferral
correctness-cost investigation (\S\ref{sec:searchtools}) both name their
criteria in a design document at a specific commit ahead of the
evaluation, rather than being justified after seeing the numbers. We
follow the same discipline in this paper's newest ablation, the
constrained-decoding investigation of \S\ref{sec:constrained}: its
criterion, including the exact iteration this section reports it
triggering, was committed before the sweep ran. Pre-registration's
case as a guard against post-hoc rationalization is well established
across empirical disciplines \citep{nosek2018preregistration}, and
this journal's own open-science initiative
\citep{mendez2019openscience} and the community's empirical standards
\citep{ralph2021standards} push the same direction for software
engineering specifically. We view pre-registration
here not as a formality borrowed from clinical trials but as an
engineering safeguard specific to iterating on a harness against a
small, noisy benchmark: without a criterion fixed in advance, a
harness developer optimizing their own lever is exactly the setting
where post-hoc rationalization of a marginal result is easiest and least
visible to a reader. We are equally explicit about \emph{coverage}:
committed criteria govern the planner iteration, the
constrained-decoding investigation, and the two solo/external baseline
measurements added during revision; the scale-curve matrix
(\S\ref{sec:curve}) and the mechanism-isolation A/B
(\S\ref{sec:mechanism}) were exploratory sweeps with no criterion
committed in advance. The title's ``pre-registered'' refers to the
adoption criteria that gate this paper's design decisions, not to
every reported comparison.

\paragraph{Registry mechanism: git-only, then git+OSF.} The three
criteria transcribed in Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} --- the planner
(\S\ref{sec:planner}), the isolated-explorer lever (not itself reported
in this paper's results), and constrained decoding
(\S\ref{sec:constrained}) --- are self-hosted: a commit to this paper's
own repository, timestamped and ordered ahead of the sweep it governs,
but recorded nowhere outside the author's control. We consider this
weaker evidence than an independent registry and disclose it as such
rather than implying otherwise. For the two solo-baseline
measurements added during revision (\texttt{gemma4:e4b} alone,
\S\ref{sec:curve}; the bare lead+executor loop,
\S\ref{sec:external}), the corresponding design documents each carry a
section drafted for an external OSF registration
(\texttt{osf.io/registries}) with the same hypothesis, design, and
analysis-plan text (\texttt{docs/gemma4-e4b-solo-baseline-design.md},
\texttt{docs/external-harness-baseline-design.md}, \S~``Registro
externo (OSF)'' in each); no external registration was ultimately
filed, so the pre-registration evidence for these two criteria is the
self-hosted record alone, with the limitation the audit below
discloses. Both sweeps ran without waiting for any external
registration, for the reason noted in each design
document (neither measurement adopts an irreversible harness change or
carries an iteration clause). A post-hoc audit of the repository,
however, found that for these two criteria even the self-hosted
mechanism fails to demonstrate precedence: the design documents were
authored in the working tree before the runs, but their first commit
(\texttt{fedbc3e}, 2026-07-18) postdates the deciding sweeps
(2026-07-13) --- the criterion text entered version control five days
after the results it governs, in a batch commit. Unlike the three
appendix criteria, whose commits verifiably precede their sweeps, these
two are therefore \emph{declared} rather than provably pre-registered,
and readers should treat their pre-registration label as unverifiable.
Two mitigating observations, offered as context rather than proof: both
measurements concluded \emph{against} the paper's original thesis (the
solo baseline and the bare-loop baseline are what forced the
framing revision and the present title), which is the direction
post-hoc framing would be least tempted to produce; and the criterion
structure (hypothesis, arms, analysis plan) matches the pattern of the
three verifiably-ordered earlier criteria. Filing the drafted OSF text
now would add only an independent timestamp that itself postdates the
runs, and would have to say so --- which is why we report this audit
instead of decorating the record. The audit --- which we
ran on ourselves and disclose here --- is the concrete argument for the
register-before-run gate the project adopts for all future criteria.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The harness-vs-scale curve}\label{sec:curve}
% Fuente: docs/sweep-curva-multiescala-2026-07-10.md

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig1_curva.pdf}
  \input{figs/fig1_curva_caption.tex}
  \label{fig:curva}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:curva} plots pass rate for four executor scales ---
llama3.2:1b, qwen2.5:3b, qwen2.5:7b, qwen3.5-coder --- under four
harness configurations, $n{=}95$ per cell, 1{,}520 runs total. The
capability lever (a lead model that proactively opens each turn) and
the context lever (a plan pre-round whose output enters the prompt as
prose) move pass rate in opposite directions, and their effect sizes
tell two different stories about what a small local model needs from
its harness.

\paragraph{The composite sits at the lead's own ceiling; the lead's
\emph{gain} decays with scale as a corollary.} Relative to each
executor's own baseline, the proactive lead adds $+70$\,pp at 1B
(19\%~$\to$~89\%), $+24$\,pp at 3B (68\%~$\to$~92\%), $+15$\,pp at 7B
(80\%~$\to$~95\%), and $-4$\,pp at the scale ceiling (98\%~$\to$~94\%,
within noise but with the expected sign: a lead weaker than its own
executor can only get in the way). The simplest model consistent with
all four rows --- established directly by the solo baseline of the
next paragraph --- is that the \texttt{+lead} arm sits at the lead
model's \emph{own} ceiling on this suite ($\approx$90--95\%)
regardless of the executor behind it. On that reading the monotonic
decay of the lead's gain is arithmetic, not a capability-transfer
phenomenon: a roughly constant composite minus a baseline that rises
with executor scale. What remains true and practically useful at the
low end is the within-executor effect: with the lead attached, a 1B
executor (89\%) outperforms the unassisted 3B (68\%) and 7B (80\%)
baselines --- though, as \S\ref{sec:discussion} quantifies, the
composite is \emph{not} cheaper per run than the lead alone or the 7B
baseline, so this is a statement about what a weak executor's
deployment can reach, not a cost argument.

\paragraph{A pre-registered solo baseline of the lead complicates the
headline, honestly.} Every lead/planner role in this paper is filled by
the same model, \texttt{gemma4:e4b} (\S\ref{sec:threats}), but no arm
in the original scale-curve sweep measures what \texttt{gemma4:e4b}
itself scores on this suite with no executor attached at all. We
closed that gap under the same pre-registration discipline as the
rest of this paper (design and criterion committed before the sweep
ran, \texttt{docs/gemma4-e4b-solo-baseline-design.md}): \texttt{
gemma4:e4b} alone reaches 87/95 = 92\% [84, 96]\% (Wilson 95\%),
\emph{statistically indistinguishable} from the 1B+lead composite
(85/95 = 89\% [82, 94]\%; Newcombe 95\% CI on the delta,
$-2$\,pp~[$-11$,~$+7$], crosses zero, with the point estimate if
anything favoring the lead alone). A pre-declared follow-up
(\texttt{docs/power-increase-2026-07-13.md}) added 10 more repetitions
to both arms to tighten this estimate: pooled at $n{=}285$, \texttt{
gemma4:e4b} alone reaches 260/285 = 91\% [87, 94]\%, the composite
253/285 = 89\% [85, 92]\%, delta $-2.5$\,pp~[$-7.5$,~$+2.5$] --- the
same null, now at roughly half the confidence interval width, ruling
out an effect larger than about 8\,pp rather than the wider band the
first pass left open. Because the pooled composite arm merges its original
95-run slice (commit \texttt{e9b841e}) with 190 power runs on the
post-hardening binary (\texttt{ec61f5e}) --- the only pooled arm in
this paper to cross that boundary --- we checked slice homogeneity for
all three pooled arms (composite 89.5\% vs.\ 88.4\%, Fisher exact
$p{=}0.85$; solo $p{=}1.00$; bare $p{=}0.85$;
\texttt{docs/emse-r2-analysis-2026-07-17.md}) and recomputed the delta
on same-commit slices only ($-2.8$\,pp [$-8.8$, $+2.6$]): the point
estimate moves by 0.3\,pp and no conclusion changes. This delta is
also the comparison in the paper most robust to task-level clustering
(\S\ref{sec:setup}): its cluster bootstrap interval, $-2.5$\,pp
[$-7.4$, $+2.1$], is essentially the Newcombe interval, because both
arms share their per-task failure pattern. The pre-registered criterion is
unambiguous here: this is exactly the pattern that triggers a framing
revision, not the pattern that would have let the original claim
stand. Figure~\ref{fig:curva}'s shaded band makes the same point
visually --- the \texttt{+lead} line sits inside \texttt{gemma4:e4b}'s
own confidence band at every executor scale we tested, never clearly
above it --- with one qualification the band inherits from
\S\ref{sec:setup}'s comparison discipline: the solo baseline was
measured in the 2026-07-13 same-day batch alongside the 1B arms, so
only the 1B comparison is a within-discipline delta; at 3B, 7B, and
the ceiling, the juxtaposition spans sweeps three days and one harness
commit apart, and is offered as visual context rather than as a tested
contrast. The 1B-executor-beats-7B-baseline comparison is therefore
better read as evidence that the harness \emph{routes} work to a
capable model efficiently than as evidence that the composite
manufactures capability beyond what that one model already has on its
own; we did not, in this sweep, isolate a synergy between the lead's
opening and the small executor's later turns that exceeds the lead's
own solo ceiling. What survives unrevised is the within-executor
result: the 1B's own baseline still moves from 19\% to 89\% when the
lead is attached, a real and large effect on that executor's usability
--- the part of the claim this paper can no longer defend is that the
resulting system's capability is attributable to the harness's
engineering beyond what routing to \texttt{gemma4:e4b} already buys on
its own. We carry this revision through the rest of the paper
(abstract, \S\ref{sec:discussion}, \S\ref{sec:threats}) rather than
soften it only here.

\paragraph{The prose plan harms at both extremes of the scale, through
the same failure mode.} Unlike the lead, the planner's prose plan is
not merely less helpful at the extremes: it is actively harmful there,
and by the same mechanism at both ends. At 1B, no task survives (0/95);
37 of its 95 failures are empty responses --- the model, handed its own
plan as assistant-role text, produces nothing at all on the following
round. At the scale ceiling, qwen3.5-coder collapses from 98\% to 49\%,
and 35 of its 48 failures are the identical empty-response signature.
A thinking model handed "its own" plan back as conversational text
stops yielding usable output in exactly the way the smallest model
does. Scale does not protect against this failure mode because the
failure is not the executor's: the chat templates these models are
served under treat a trailing assistant message as a \emph{prefill} to
continue rather than a turn to answer, so neither model was asked for
a fresh response at all (\S\ref{sec:planner} shows this in the
templates themselves). (7B is the informative
exception --- see below.) We diagnose and repair this degeneration under
pre-registration in \S\ref{sec:planner}.

\paragraph{The lead absorbs the planner's damage at every scale.} When
both levers are active, the composition is statistically
indistinguishable from the lead alone at all four executor scales:
$+0.0$\,pp at 1B (88.4\% vs.\ 88.4\%, pre-registered clean re-run ---
see below), $-3.2$\,pp at 3B (88\% vs.\
92\%), $0.0$\,pp at 7B (95\%~=~95\%), and $-2.1$\,pp at the ceiling
(92\% vs.\ 94\%) --- every Newcombe interval crosses zero. A lever that
costs an executor its entire pass rate on its own (0/95 at 1B) costs
nothing measurable once a capable model opens the turn. This is the
pinned-ceiling reading again from a different angle: with the lead
attached the system sits at the lead's own ceiling, and what the
executor is doing --- including following a plan delivered in a format
that destroys it unassisted --- stops being the binding constraint.

The 1B cell in that comparison corrects an earlier version of this
analysis, and we report the correction's full history rather than only
its result. The value originally computed for the composed arm at 1B
was 61.1\% (58/95), which supported the opposite reading: that the
lead only partially repairs the damage at the smallest scale. An audit
of the sweep's own logs
(\texttt{docs/curve-transport-audit-2026-07-18.md}) found that 30 of
that cell's 95 runs died of transport failures --- the engine logged
``planner call failed; proceeding without a plan'' with a connection
error in 30 distinct sessions, and those 30 runs scored 0/30, making
them dead rows rather than degraded ones (the 65 runs that reached the
server score 58/65 = 89.2\% [79.4, 94.7]\%). Rather than leave the
correction as an analytical exclusion over already-seen data, we
re-measured the cell under a pre-registered design
(\texttt{docs/rerun-contaminated-cells-design-2026-07-18.md}: the
composed cell together with its \texttt{+lead} comparison cell in one
two-arm sweep, so the delta is within-sweep by construction; a
committed validity rule --- no arm may exceed 2\% transport failures
--- decides whether the sweep counts before any number is read). The
clean re-run (190 runs, 2026-07-19; \emph{zero} transport failures in
either arm, every failure a semantic assertion) gives composed 84/95 =
88.4\% [80.4, 93.4]\% and lead-alone 84/95 = 88.4\% --- a delta of
exactly $+0.0$\,pp, Newcombe [$-9.4$, $+9.4$]. The pre-declared
reading for this outcome applies: the lead absorbs the planner's
damage \emph{completely} at every scale measured, including the
smallest, and the earlier ``partial repair at 1B'' reading is
withdrawn as a transport artifact. The 1B numbers in the opening
paragraph above are the re-run's; the contaminated attempt and the
intermediate exclusion-based estimate are both preserved in the
repository.

\paragraph{The 7B exception is informative, not noise.} 7B is the only
scale where the (undamaged, pre-iteration) prose plan helps: baseline
\texttt{error\_recovery} is 0/15, entirely semantic failures
(\texttt{assertion\_text}/\texttt{assertion\_files}, not malformed tool
calls), and the plan corrects 12 of them. This suggests a narrow
capability window --- large enough to follow a plan without degenerating
into silence, not yet large enough to not need one --- rather than a
counterexample to the scale-harm pattern above; at 7B the lead alone
already reaches the same 95\% without the planner's added risk
elsewhere in the curve.

\paragraph{At the ceiling, the lead is also the faster arm.} For
qwen3.5-coder, \texttt{+lead} averages 19.8s per run against the
baseline's 23.7s: gemma4:e4b opening the first rounds is cheaper than
having the reasoning-heavy coder model think through them itself. We did
not design for this and do not treat it as a general result, but it is
a striking secondary reading of the same lever --- a light lead can act
as an accelerant for a heavy executor, not only a capability patch for a
weak one.

\paragraph{Replication.} The 3B baseline (68\% here) sits in a
67--72\% band across four independent sweeps in this paper (71.6\% in
the mechanism-isolation ablation of \S\ref{sec:mechanism}'s underlying
matrix, 67.4\% in the three-arm A/B of \S\ref{sec:mechanism}, 70.5\% in
an earlier A/B not reported here in detail), and its \texttt{+lead} arm
similarly clusters at 92--92.6\% across three sweeps; \texttt{
error\_recovery} baseline stays at the floor (0--3/15) throughout the
qwen2.5 series. We read this as evidence that the curve's qualitative
shape, not just its point estimates, is stable to re-measurement.

\subsection{An external comparison point: composition versus
engineering}\label{sec:external}
% Fuente: docs/external-harness-baseline-design.md,
% docs/sweep-external-bare-lead-2026-07-13.json

Every comparison so far is \braze{} against itself under different lever
configurations. To close that gap, we built, under the same
pre-registration discipline as the rest of this paper
(\texttt{docs/external-harness-baseline-design.md}, criterion committed
before this section's sweep ran), a lead+executor tool-calling loop
implemented entirely from scratch --- not \braze{}'s
\texttt{EscalatingBackend} decorator, not \texttt{braze\_engine::Engine}
--- with none of Table~\ref{tab:levers}'s other levers: no textual
rescue, no observation collapse, no tool deferral, no post-edit check,
a four-sentence generic system prompt instead of \braze{}'s own. It
keeps only what a minimal lead+executor composition requires: the same
proactive-opening split (\texttt{lead\_turns=3}), the same six local
tools executed directly, and the same sandbox/permission-denial posture
every other row in this paper uses, so a difference in outcome is
attributable to the orchestration loop, not to different tools or a
more permissive safety boundary.

On the 1B row --- the scale \S\ref{sec:curve} identifies as most
informative --- this bare loop reaches 84/95 = 88.4\% [80.4, 93.4]\%,
statistically indistinguishable from both \braze{}'s full 1B+lead
composite (89.5\% [81.7, 94.2]\%; delta $+1.1$\,pp, Newcombe 95\% CI
[$-8.2$, $+10.3$]) and from \texttt{gemma4:e4b} on its own
(91.6\%; delta $-3.2$\,pp, Newcombe 95\% CI [$-12.1$, $+5.7$]). Three
independent measurements --- the helper model alone, \braze{}'s fully
engineered composite, and a from-scratch reimplementation of just the
composition pattern --- land in the same 88--92\% band, mutually
indistinguishable at $n{=}95$. A pre-declared power-increase pass
(\texttt{docs/power-increase-2026-07-13.md}) added 10 more repetitions
to all three arms: pooled at $n{=}285$ each, the bare loop settles at
249/285 = 87.4\% [83.0, 90.7]\%, the composite at 253/285 = 88.8\%
[84.6, 91.9]\%, and \texttt{gemma4:e4b} alone at 260/285 = 91.2\%
[87.4, 94.0]\% --- the same three-way null, at roughly half the
confidence-interval width (delta composite$-$bare $+1.4$\,pp
[$-4.0$,$+6.8$]; bare$-$solo $-3.9$\,pp [$-9.0$,$+1.3$]; composite$-$solo
$-2.5$\,pp [$-7.5$,$+2.5$]), with all three point estimates within
1\,pp of where the first pass put them. Nothing in this data separates "the
composite achieves something beyond \texttt{gemma4:e4b}'s own ceiling"
from "neither the lead/executor pattern itself nor \braze{}'s
additional engineering adds a measurable gain over that ceiling, on
this suite, at this scale" --- the confidence
intervals, now tighter, are still compatible with a true difference of up
to roughly 5--9\,pp in either direction, so this remains a failure to detect
an effect, not evidence the effect is exactly zero; the replication's
main contribution is ruling out a \emph{larger} hidden effect, not
resolving the sign. One qualification the task-level cluster bootstrap
of \S\ref{sec:setup} adds: because the bare loop's failures concentrate
in specific tasks (the multi-step/distractor tail) rather than
spreading across the suite, deltas involving the bare arm are far less
precise under task resampling than their Newcombe intervals suggest
(bare$-$solo [$-17.5$, $+11.2$]\,pp; composite$-$bare [$-13.3$,
$+15.8$]\,pp) --- the ``rules out a larger effect'' reading survives
clustering only for the composite$-$solo comparison ([$-7.4$,
$+2.1$]\,pp, \S\ref{sec:curve}); for the bare loop the honest statement
is directional agreement at the point estimate with wide task-level
uncertainty. What the bare loop
does establish is that the lead/executor pattern itself is robust
without any of \braze{}'s scaffolding: in the original $n{=}95$ pass it
solves every \texttt{single\_tool} and \texttt{no\_tool} task (35/35,
15/15) and degrades only on the multi-step/distractor skills where the
1B executor is already weak on its own, with zero schema failures
across all 95 runs.

\S\ref{sec:threats} discusses what this arm's implementation still
shares with \braze{} (the underlying \texttt{braze\_model} HTTP client
and \texttt{braze\_tools\_local} tool implementations, not the
orchestration logic under test) and what a fully independent comparison
--- e.g.\ against a third-party harness like Pi \citep{pi-dev}, which we
identified as a candidate during this work but did not implement ---
would additionally require.

\subsection{An external task anchor: a pre-registered BFCL
subset}\label{sec:bfcl}
% Fuente: docs/sweep-bfcl-anchor-2026-07-18.md,
% docs/bfcl-anchor-design-2026-07-18.md, docs/bfcl-anchor-RESUME.md

Every result so far is measured on tasks we authored, and
\S\ref{sec:threats} names the sharpest consequence: circularity
between the suite and the harness tuned against it. This section
anchors the paper's central claims on tasks we did not write. The
design was committed before the sweep ran
(\texttt{docs/bfcl-anchor-design-2026-07-18.md}): 60 tasks sampled
deterministically (seed 42) from the Berkeley Function-Calling
Leaderboard \citep{bfcl}, 20 each from the \texttt{simple},
\texttt{multiple}, and \texttt{irrelevance} categories; an offline
grader in the BFCL style (AST equality over function name \emph{and}
arguments, per-category acceptable-answer sets) committed alongside
the design; four readings (E1--E4) pre-declared; and a validity rule
--- no arm may exceed 2\% transport failures --- committed after
three earlier attempts at this sweep were invalidated by
infrastructure, not by models (an OOM from model stacking on the
inference host, a degraded-network run without transport retries,
and a host-contention run $8\times$ slower than baseline; all three
are preserved in the repository for disclosure). Five arms
$\times$ 300 runs each (60 tasks $\times$ 5 repetitions) give 1{,}500
runs; the first valid attempt passed the rule in every arm (worst
arm: 1.0\% transport).

the expected tool --- the identity proxy \braze{}'s own runner can
grade live) and \emph{offline} (the committed AST grader --- the
level that counts, and the one all claims below use).

score.

\paragraph{The baseline ordering transfers (E1).} Offline,
\texttt{qwen3.5-coder} $>$ \texttt{qwen2.5:3b} $>$

versus 97.9\% authored, which we read as BFCL exercising a wider
variety of argument formats than our suite does.

\paragraph{The central lever replicates (E2).} On the authored suite
the proactive lead moves the 1B executor by $+70.5$\,pp. On the
anchor, offline: \textbf{$+72.0$\,pp} (20.7\% $\to$ 92.7\%), Newcombe

within 1.5\,pp of the authored effect. The paper's headline lever
transfers to a benchmark we did not author.

\paragraph{The ceiling-pinning null replicates (E3).} As on the
authored suite, the composite is statistically indistinguishable from
the lead alone: composite $-$ solo $= +0.0$\,pp offline (Newcombe

down --- consistent with the pinned-ceiling reading of
\S\ref{sec:curve}.

\paragraph{The identity$\to$arguments gap quantifies the thesis
(E4).} The difference between the online and offline levels is, per
model, a one-number summary of why argument-level harness support
exists: $+47.0$\,pp for \texttt{llama3.2:1b} --- the 1B names the

models. Naming the tool is not the bottleneck at 1B; constructing
its arguments is, and that is precisely the failure class the
schema-repair, rescue, and escalation levers target. The gap is also
\emph{negative} for one arm ($-9.0$\,pp, \texttt{qwen3.5-coder}):
the thinking model produces correct calls that the identity proxy
fails to credit, a grading-validity caveat taken up in
\S\ref{sec:threats}.

\texttt{gemma4:e4b} holds 88\% --- consistent with BFCL's own recent
repositioning of this category as a hallucination measurement.

\subsection{A repository-level anchor: a pre-registered SWE-bench Lite

\S\ref{sec:bfcl} anchors the central lever on function-calling tasks;
this section anchors it on \emph{repository-level software-engineering
tasks} --- real issues, real repositories, outcomes graded by each
project's own tests. The design was committed before the driver was
implemented or any run executed
(\texttt{docs/swebench-lite-anchor-design-2026-07-19.md}): 20
instances sampled deterministically (seed 42 over sorted instance

pre-declared readings. Grading is the \emph{official} SWE-bench

\paragraph{The 1B alone is a floor of the most informative kind.}
Zero non-empty patches in 40 runs, at a median of 4 seconds per run:
the unassisted 1B does not fail at repository tasks so much as never
engage them --- it answers in prose without attempting an edit. This is
the boundary the paper's claims need stated: no reliability lever
measured in this paper turns a model that cannot engage a repository
into one that can.

\paragraph{The lead lever moves behavior outside noise, and resolution
directionally.} We report two layers separately rather than blend
them. Behaviorally, the composite produces non-empty patches in 6/40
runs where the 1B alone produced 0/40 (Fisher exact $p{=}0.026$) ---
the lever's turn-opening converts ``never edits'' into ``edits.'' At

(\texttt{django\_\_django-14382}) resolved in \emph{both}
repetitions, consistency rather than a sampling fluke. The honest
compound statement: the lever transfers its behavioral effect to
repository tasks with statistical support, and its resolution effect
only directionally.

\paragraph{The pinned ceiling replicates on real tasks.} The
composite (2/40) is indistinguishable from the lead alone (3/40,
$p{=}1.0$), at absolute levels (5--7.5\%) that are exactly where the
lead's own ceiling sits on tasks this hard. The paper's central
pattern --- the composite lives at the lead's ceiling; the 1B neither
adds nor subtracts --- holds on execution-graded software-engineering
tasks, not just on the suites where we first measured it. As a
reference point the benchmark's public leaderboards rarely cover: a
quantized 8B-MoE helper on CPU resolves two distinct SWE-bench Lite
instances.

Costs replicate directionally too (median wall 4/50/158\,s across the
three arms --- the composite is $\sim$3$\times$ faster per run than the
lead alone, consistent with \S\ref{sec:curve}'s accelerant reading).
Threats specific to this anchor --- the 4{,}000-character issue-text
truncation (5 instances affected, recorded per run), pre-training
contamination of these public repositories (affects levels, not
between-arm comparisons), and the driver living outside
\texttt{braze-bench} (compensated by recording the same identity
fields: harness commit, instance list, model digests, server version)
--- are stated in the committed design and \S\ref{sec:threats}. Raw
runs, predictions, and the official harness's reports are in the
repository.

\subsection{Isolating the mechanism: proactive opening, not reactive
escalation}\label{sec:mechanism}
% Fuente: docs/sweep-lead-3brazos-2026-07-10.md

The lead lever in \S\ref{sec:curve} bundles two mechanisms Goose's
reactive lead/worker pattern \citep{goose} leaves conflated
(\S\ref{sec:related}): the lead \emph{proactively} opens the first
\texttt{lead\_turns} rounds of every turn by default, and can
separately \emph{reactively} re-escalate back to the lead after
consecutive tool-call failures. Reactive escalation is armed but rarely
fires when proactive opening is also active, so the curve's numbers
cannot by themselves attribute the gain to one mechanism or the other.
We isolate them with a three-arm A/B on qwen2.5:3b against the same
lead (gemma4:e4b), 285 runs, $n{=}95$ per arm: a baseline with no lead,
proactive opening (the default), and \emph{purely reactive} escalation
(\texttt{+ablate:lead-turns=0}, which disables proactive opening while
keeping the reactive trigger armed).

Proactive opening reaches 92.6\% [85.6, 96.4]; purely reactive
escalation reaches 75.8\% [66.3, 83.3]; baseline is 67.4\% [57.4, 76.0].
The proactive and reactive intervals do not overlap --- the entire gain
the curve attributes to "the lead" is attributable to proactive
opening, not reactive escalation. Reactive escalation's own gain over
baseline ($+8.4$\,pp) has confidence intervals that overlap substantially
with baseline's; at $n{=}95$ per arm, that improvement alone is not
distinguishable from noise.

The reason is structural, not incidental. \texttt{error\_recovery} is
the skill where proactive opening helps most (0/15~$\to$~14/15) and
where reactive escalation helps least (0/15~$\to$~2/15): all 13 of the
reactive arm's failures on this skill occurred with \emph{zero}
escalations triggered. The reactive trigger counts consecutive
\emph{failed} tool observations, but the dominant failure mode on this
skill is executing successfully on the wrong target --- reading an
error message and answering incorrectly, or editing the wrong file
without any tool call itself failing. No mechanical signal accumulates,
so the lead never takes over, and the task fails regardless. When the
reactive trigger \emph{does} fire, it works: all 7 runs with at least
one escalation in this sweep passed. This is not evidence of a broken
mechanism but of a narrow one --- reactive escalation only sees the band
of failures that manifest as consecutive rejected or erroneous tool
calls, and pays roughly $2\times$ the baseline latency (11.0s vs.\ 5.5s)
for an unreliable $+8$\,pp when it does trigger, against proactive
opening's $5.9\times$ latency cost (32.2s) for a robust $+25$\,pp.
Proactive opening also eliminates a failure class the reactive trigger
cannot see at all: turns that abort with an empty response before any
tool call is even attempted accumulate no failed observations to
trigger on, and occur only in the arms where the small executor opens
the turn itself (5 of 95 baseline runs, 7 of 95 reactive --- an eighth
reactive run-error is a timeout, not an empty response --- and 0 of 95
proactive).

\subsection{A negative result, diagnosed and repaired}\label{sec:planner}
% Fuente: docs/sweep-planner-ab-2026-07-11.md; docs/sweep-matriz-4brazos-2026-07-10.md

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figs/fig2_rescate.pdf}
  \input{figs/fig2_rescate_caption.tex}
  \label{fig:rescate}
\end{figure}

The planner's history in this project is a case study in what
pre-registration buys, told against ourselves. An initial A/B measuring
the planner (plan rendered as the executor's own assistant-role text)
against a pre-registered adoption criterion came back negative but not
decisively so, and the criterion --- written before that sweep ran ---
kept it opt-in with a named iteration path rather than either removing
it outright or rationalizing a marginal result into a keep. A follow-up
four-arm matrix made the picture decisive: the planner alone was not
neutral but actively harmful, $-22$\,pp against baseline, and the
failure mode was degeneration --- empty responses --- not disobedience of
the plan's content. The scale curve of \S\ref{sec:curve} then showed the
same degeneration signature at \emph{both} ends of the executor scale
(1B: 0/95, 37 empty responses; the ceiling: 98\%~$\to$~49\%, 35 of 48
failures empty), converting a scale-specific curiosity into a mechanism
claim: handing a model its own plan back as conversational text
reliably yields turns with no usable output, independent of how
capable the model is.

\paragraph{The mechanism is the chat template's prefill convention, not
the model's disposition.} An earlier version of this paper attributed
that signature to the executor --- a small model ``going silent'' when
handed its own plan. Two checks against material we already had, run
after a reviewer asked us to exclude cheaper explanations, relocate the
mechanism from the model to the interface between the harness and the
serving layer.

The first rules out the obvious alternative. The engine emits a warning
whenever a completion stops because it exhausted its token budget
(\texttt{stop\_reason} of \texttt{length} or \texttt{max\_tokens}); that
warning fires three times in the whole 1B slice and never in the qwen
slice, while the empty-response turns generated 47--594 and 44--619
tokens respectively against a budget of 4{,}096. Budget exhaustion ---
including the reasoning-channel variant plausible for a thinking-tuned
model --- is therefore not what produced these turns
(\texttt{docs/curve-transport-audit-2026-07-18.md}).

The second identifies what did. In the original design a planned turn
renders as \texttt{[user: task, assistant: plan]}, so the last message
before generation carries the assistant role --- and chat templates
treat that case as a \emph{prefill}. Inspecting the templates the
serving layer actually applies (\texttt{ollama show --template};
\texttt{docs/empty-response-template-probe-partA-2026-07-18.md}) makes
this explicit rather than inferred. Llama~3.2's template emits its
end-of-turn token only when the assistant message is \emph{not} last
(\texttt{\{\{ if not \$last \}\}<|eot\_id|>\{\{ end \}\}}), and
Qwen~2.5's states the same rule from the other side: the generation
header is emitted \texttt{\{\{ if and (ne .Role "assistant") \$last
\}\}} --- if and only if the last message is not the assistant's. Under
the original delivery, then, neither model was asked to answer. Each
was asked to \emph{continue} a plan that was already complete, inside a
turn the template deliberately left open. The user-role delivery of the
repair below closes that turn and opens a fresh one, which is why it
works, and why it works at both ends of the scale.

This reframes the finding rather than weakening it. What
\S\ref{sec:curve} measures as a scale-independent collapse is not a
claim about what small models do with plans; it is a claim about what
happens when a harness injects context in the assistant role against a
template family whose prefill convention is close to universal. That is
both more actionable --- any harness can check its own templates with
one command --- and more portable, since it predicts the same failure
for models we never tested. Two limits are worth stating. The templates
of \texttt{qwen3.5-coder} and \texttt{gemma4:e4b} are rendered by
built-in Ollama renderers rather than inspectable Go templates, so the
documentary evidence covers two of the three executors that exhibited
the collapse, and we do not extrapolate to the ceiling model.

A pre-registered behavioural probe
(\texttt{docs/empty-response-template-probe-design-2026-07-18.md};
results \texttt{-partB-2026-07-19.md}) then tested the account
directly: 80 matched \texttt{/api/chat} requests against the same
serving stack, no harness in between, crossing the role of the final
message (assistant-last vs.\ user-last) with a real
\texttt{gemma4:e4b} plan in two states (complete vs.\ cut
mid-sentence), $n{=}10$ per cell. For \texttt{llama3.2:1b} all three
pre-declared template signatures land at once: the complete plan as
the final \emph{assistant} message yields empty content in 10/10
requests with \texttt{eval\_count}~$=1$ --- the model emits an
immediate end-of-turn token, exactly what ``continue a message that
already looks finished'' predicts --- while the same plan cut
mid-sentence is \emph{continued} (0/10 empty, 147--253 tokens) and the
same complete plan delivered as \emph{user} context is answered (0/10
empty). For this model the mechanism claim is therefore behavioural,
not just documentary. \texttt{qwen3.5-coder} diverges, per the
pre-declared per-model reading: in the minimal probe, role alone does
\emph{not} reproduce its collapse (assistant-last, complete plan: 0/10
empty), so its sweep-time degeneration involves more than the bare
prefill effect --- consistent with its template being uninspectable
and with the probe's declared limit (a minimal prompt is not
\braze{}'s full prompt), and we scope the mechanism claim to the
executor where it verified. The probe also settles the
where-did-the-tokens-go question for the 1B in the minimal setting
(nowhere: one token) while leaving the 47--594-token empties of the
full-prompt sweep as an unclosed accounting, which we state rather
than extrapolate; one probe cell (coder, complete plan, user-last)
returned no data --- all ten requests exceeded the probe's own
5-minute cap, a probe-harness limit disclosed as such.

The pre-registered iteration (commit \texttt{e8c7d3f}) changed exactly
two things: the plan is now delivered as \emph{user}-role context
instead of assistant-role text, and single-step plans (which carry no
real structure to follow) are discarded rather than injected. Measured
against the two damaged reference points --- qwen2.5:3b and
qwen3.5-coder, both at 49\% under the old delivery --- the iteration
recovers both, but by different deliveries at different scales
(Figure~\ref{fig:rescate}). For the capable executor, re-rendering the
same prose plan as user-role context eliminates the collapse: 95.8\%
raw against an 86.3\% same-sweep baseline. That raw $+10$\,pp delta,
however, did not survive a closer look at the baseline arm: a
transient network event cost the coder \emph{baseline} 10 of its 13
failures (requests that never reached the model;
\texttt{docs/emse-r2-analysis-2026-07-17.md}). An earlier revision of
this section corrected for that with an analytical exclusion; we have
since replaced the exclusion with data. All three coder arms were
re-measured under a pre-registered clean-re-run design
(\texttt{docs/rerun-contaminated-cells-design-2026-07-18.md}, Bloque
2; same 2\%-transport validity rule as \S\ref{sec:bfcl}; the first
re-run attempt was itself invalidated by that rule after exposing a
circuit-breaker bug in the harness --- a cancelled half-open probe
poisoned two arms --- which we fixed, disclosed, and preserved before
re-running). The clean sweep (285 runs, 2026-07-19) has \emph{zero}
transport failures under the audit criterion in all three arms; its
eleven \texttt{model\_backend\_error} rows are the model emitting
malformed XML tool-call syntax that the textual-rescue parser
surfaces as a stream error (wall-clock 18--78\,s --- model failures,
not infrastructure; that the rescue ladder converts them into
backend errors rather than degrading is itself a harness finding we
record for future work). On clean data: coder baseline 93/95 =
97.9\% [92.6, 99.4]\% --- now agreeing exactly with the scale
curve's 97.9\%, resolving the apparent cross-sweep swing --- and the
user-role arm 90/95 = 94.7\% [88.3, 97.7]\%, delta $-3.2$\,pp
[$-9.8$, $+2.9$]: the fixed delivery restores the ceiling executor
from its 49\% collapse to its own baseline band, but does not beat
it. The task-list delivery at the ceiling, however, \emph{diverges}
from the earlier exclusion-based estimate, and we report it as the
pre-registered design requires: 84/95 = 88.4\%, delta $-9.5$\,pp
[$-17.6$, $-2.2$] --- outside zero, on the harmful side (its failure
mix: four timeouts, the XML-rescue errors above, and one tool
assertion). At the ceiling, converting the plan into typed task
state does not merely fail to help; it costs measurable pass rate.
For the small executor, whose arms
in this sweep show no transport failures, prose alone is not enough ---
user-role rendering alone reaches only 63\%, marginally under its
67\% baseline --- but delivering the plan as \braze{}'s typed task list
(\S\ref{sec:harness}), re-injected as a compact summary each round
rather than left as text to re-interpret, reaches 79\%
($+11.6$\,pp, Newcombe 95\% [$-1.0$, $+23.7$] --- the largest movement
in the sweep, its interval marginally crossing zero), with
\texttt{error\_recovery} 3/15~$\to$~10/15. The pattern is scale-
dependent in a specific and interpretable way: a capable model needs
its plan rendered in a role it will not mistake for its own voice; a
small model needs the plan as re-injected structured state it does not
have to re-parse from prose at all.

Applied to the letter, the pre-registered criterion adopts
\emph{none} of the deliveries, and we report that literal verdict
together with a dated deviation note rather than reframe it. The
criterion (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}, text~(1)) requires the target
pair --- \texttt{multi\_step} plus \texttt{error\_recovery} --- to
move outside
the baseline's Wilson interval without degrading the other three
skills, and counts the planner's token cost toward the verdict. On the
3B executor, the task-list delivery moves the target pair from 12/30
to 17/30 --- an improvement, but \emph{inside} the baseline's Wilson
interval [24.6, 57.7]\% (56.7\% against a 57.7\% upper bound); the
aggregate delta marginally crosses zero ($+11.6$\,pp [$-1.0$,
$+23.7$]); \texttt{multi\_step} itself regresses (9/15~$\to$~7/15)
while \texttt{error\_recovery} improves (3/15~$\to$~10/15); the three
guardrail skills do not degrade; and the delivery costs roughly
$2\times$ the baseline's input tokens (5{,}352 vs.\ 2{,}635 per run)
and $4\times$ its output tokens --- a cost the criterion explicitly
counts. The user-role delivery at 3B fails more clearly (target pair
10/30, below baseline); at the ceiling, the user-role delivery
recovers the collapse without measurably exceeding its baseline
($-3.2$\,pp, crossing zero) and the task-list delivery lands below
its baseline outright ($-9.5$\,pp [$-17.6$, $-2.2$], clean re-run) ---
reinforcing the rejection from a second direction: the delivery with
the largest (marginal) movement at 3B is measurably harmful at the
scale ceiling. \emph{Deviation note
(2026-07-17)}: the criterion's rejection clause prescribes removing
the lever; we keep it implemented and opt-in --- never default ---
because the iteration's diagnostic finding is this section's actual
contribution, and deleting the lever would delete the apparatus that
measured it. What this section establishes is therefore narrower than
an adoption: planning's \emph{harm} was an artifact of delivery
format, repairable and repaired at both scales; planning's
\emph{benefit}, at the scales and sample sizes measured here, does not
clear the bar this project committed to before the data came in. One
arm of this sweep (3B, task-list delivery) was re-run after its
original attempt reported 35\%: 58 of its 62 failures on that attempt
were transient Nitro network errors concentrated entirely in that arm,
not a property of the task-list delivery; the clean re-run is the 79\%
reported above. The same network event's lighter footprint on the
coder arms (10, 2, and 8 transport failures on baseline, user-role,
and task-list respectively) is what the clean re-run above replaces;
we disclose the contaminated original, the intermediate
exclusion-based estimate, the invalidated first re-run attempt, and
the clean re-measurement, rather than silently substitute any of
them.

\braze{}'s two-level tool deferral (\S\ref{sec:harness}) hides a
provider's tools behind a single \texttt{search\_tools} meta-tool once
the registry exceeds a stub-count threshold, so the prompt carries only
names and one-line summaries instead of every tool's full schema. We
evaluate it on a companion six-task suite
(\texttt{crates/braze-bench/suites/tool-search.toml}) mirroring the
base suite's skills --- \texttt{noisy\_read\_file},
\texttt{noisy\_grep}, \texttt{noisy\_write\_file} (single-tool),
\texttt{noisy\_no\_tool}, \texttt{noisy\_multi\_step}, and
\texttt{noisy\_distractor} --- whose registry carries the six real
local tools plus 200 synthetic noise tools standing in for an
MCP-gateway-scale catalog, comparing deferral on (search-based, the
noise catalog hidden) against off (all 206 tools listed), on
qwen2.5:3b and qwen2.5:7b, $n{=}90$ per arm (6 tasks $\times$ 15
repetitions) after three successive sweeps at increasing repetition
count converged on a harness-hardened binary.

The token savings replicate cleanly across all three sweeps and are not
in question: deferral cuts input tokens $5.4\times$ (3B) to $6.1\times$
(7B), with roughly $2\times$ less wall-clock. At the scale of a real
tool gateway --- 1,500+ tools, on the order of 30K tokens of inventory
--- this is not an optimization but an enabling condition: the
full-inventory arm would not fit an 8K local context window at all.

The correctness question is where this section's history matters. An
earlier pass at $n{=}30$ with a looser text-match assertion reported
statistically indistinguishable pass rates between the two arms --- "the
deferral is free." Repeating the sweep at $n{=}90$ against a hardened
harness (a stricter assertion that no longer accepts a model's
intermediate narration as a final answer, a context-budget fix that
measures the prompt the model actually sees post-deferral rather than
the full pre-deferral catalog, and a warm-up pass eliminating a
first-task cold-start confound) does not replicate that null result:
deferral costs $-24$\,pp on the 3B (58.9\% [49,68] vs.\ 83.3\% [74,90])
and $-13$\,pp on the 7B (83.3\% [74,90] vs.\ 96.7\% [91,99]), with
confidence intervals that no longer overlap. The "no correctness cost"
claim was an artifact of the smaller sample and the looser assertion, not
a property of the mechanism.

Having a real cost, the natural first hypothesis is that the deferred
arm sometimes needs a hidden tool and fails to find it. It is not: the
gate instrumenting direct calls to a still-hidden tool by name fired
zero times across all 360 runs in this sweep, the third consecutive
sweep to report this. Nor is it distraction by catalog size ---
\texttt{noisy\_distractor} passes 15/15 in \emph{every} arm regardless
of whether 6 or 206 tools are listed, replicating this paper's earlier
finding that plausibility, not volume, is what distracts a small model.
The cost falls instead on tasks with no tool-search component at all:
\texttt{noisy\_no\_tool} (a pure arithmetic question, zero tool calls
expected) drops from 15/15 to 5/15 on the 3B under deferral, and
\texttt{noisy\_multi\_step} (read two files, sum, write the result)
drops from 12/15 to 0/15 on the 7B.

We probed both anomalies directly with preserved-session transcripts
before accepting either as a finding. For \texttt{noisy\_no\_tool}
("what is $7\times8$?"), three matched-seed conditions isolate the
cause: with only the six real local tools and no search stub, the 3B
gets the arithmetic right 11/15 times (this task is already marginal
for this model in a clean prompt); adding the \texttt{search\_tools}
stub --- whose summary text mentions "raster clip," "send email," and
similar unrelated content sitting next to an arithmetic question ---
drops it to 5/15, with one failing transcript answering literally
"5.5"; adding the full 206 inert noise tools back \emph{restores} 15/15.
For \texttt{noisy\_multi\_step} on the 7B, transcripts under deferral
show the model writing the literal string \texttt{"10\textbackslash n20"}
to the output file in 4 of 5 sampled runs (a manually-inspected sample,
not the arm's full $n{=}15$, so this is illustrative of a mechanism, not
a measured rate of it) --- it read both numbers correctly and wrote them
correctly, but never computed their sum, and its final answer describes
"the numbers 10 and 20" rather than their total; the same seeds under
the full-catalog arm compute 30 correctly. Neither probe shows a
findability problem (both models locate and read/write the right files)
or a distraction-by-volume problem (more noise tools \emph{helps} in the
first probe, the opposite of a dilution account). The stronger of the
two probes --- \texttt{noisy\_no\_tool}'s three matched-seed conditions,
45 controlled runs in total --- directly isolates the mechanism we call
\emph{compositional brittleness}: whether a small model correctly
composes a multi-step arithmetic result depends on content elsewhere in
the prompt that is semantically irrelevant to the task, in a direction
ordinary intuition does not predict. The \texttt{noisy\_multi\_step}
transcripts are consistent with the same account but, on their own
5-transcript sample, corroborate it rather than independently
establish it.

The practical implication is narrower than either "deferral is free" or
"deferral costs correctness" as a blanket rule: it remains the only
option once an inventory does not fit context, but for models at 7B and
below with an inventory that \emph{does} fit, this data argues against
enabling it by default --- consistent with \braze{}'s existing
conservative threshold. For this paper's thesis, the more general
reading is the one we take forward to \S\ref{sec:discussion}: harness
effects on small models are non-monotonic in ways that are not
predictable from mechanism-level reasoning about what a lever
\emph{should} do, and must be measured rather than assumed.

\subsection{Secondary levers and ablations}\label{sec:ablations}

Not every lever this paper implements is a headline result, and
reporting only the ones that moved the number would misrepresent how
often each actually fires. The per-model-family textual rescue
(\S\ref{sec:harness}, motivated by the Qwen3-Coder-Next findings of
\S\ref{sec:related}) recovers zero tool calls across every sweep against
the Qwen executors in this suite --- 570+ runs across two independent
sweeps report \texttt{rescued\_tool\_calls = 0}: these models' failures
in our suite are schema or semantic failures, not native-template leaks
the rescue ladder would catch. We report this as a finding about
\emph{when} the lever activates, not as evidence against it: in
separate, less formal usability testing against \texttt{z-ai/glm-5.2}
served via OpenRouter, the same model leaked its own tag-based tool-call
grammar (\texttt{<tool\_call>name<arg\_key>...</arg\_key>...}) uncaught
by the rescue ladder's three existing grammars, both in the main
completion round and, via a second, independent code path, in the
tools-free summary fallback that runs after an empty response --- the
lever's coverage is real but was, at the time, incomplete for a fourth
template family, and the two gaps were closed once identified. The
lesson generalizes the pattern documented in
\S\ref{sec:mechanism}'s reactive-escalation trigger: several of this
harness's levers have narrow, template- or trigger-specific coverage
that a single benchmark suite against a handful of model families can
easily fail to exercise, which argues for treating a lever's
\emph{observed activation rate} as a first-class quantity to report,
not an implementation detail.

The remaining levers in Table~\ref{tab:levers} --- the unablated
observation collapse, the post-edit compiler-feedback guardrail,
best-of-$n$ voting, and the mid-turn harness notes --- are implemented,
covered by unit tests, and shipped as \texttt{braze}'s default behavior,
but this paper does not isolate any of them with a dedicated ablation
sweep; we report this honestly as a gap rather than imply coverage we do
not have (\S\ref{sec:threats}).

\subsection{Constrained decoding: syntax-level control versus
harness-level rescue}\label{sec:constrained}

This paper's newest lever is a second, largely independent research
question from the levers above: instead of recovering a malformed tool
call after the fact (the textual rescue ladder), can the decoder be
constrained so a malformed call is syntactically impossible in the
first place? We report it under its own pre-registration
(\S\ref{sec:setup}). \texttt{OllamaBackend} can advertise tools either
natively (\texttt{tools} field) or via a prompt-rendered addendum
instructing the model to answer with a JSON \emph{envelope}
(\texttt{\{"action": "tool\_call"\textbar"final\_answer", ...\}}), and,
in the latter mode, additionally \emph{constrain} the decoder to that
envelope's JSON schema via Ollama's structured-output support --- making
a malformed tool-call syntactically impossible rather than repairing it
after the fact, the opposite end of the inference-vs-harness spectrum
from the textual rescue ladder above. Against the pre-registered
criterion (adopt if constrained decoding beats the native baseline by
$\ge$10\,pp, outside noise, on at least one weak executor, \emph{and}
beats the unconstrained prompt-rendering arm by any margin, with the
rescue-ladder mechanism check near zero), neither of the two weak
executors we test (llama3.2:1b, gemma4:e2b) cleared the bar on the
first pass: llama3.2:1b's raw gain over native ($+12.6$\,pp) is only
marginally outside noise --- 95\% Newcombe [$+0.4$, $+24.4$]
uncorrected, a lower bound close enough to zero that the same interval
computed with continuity correction lands on the other side of it
([$-0.3$, $+25.1$]); we report the uncorrected value for consistency
with the rest of the paper (\S\ref{sec:setup}) and treat the
comparison as marginal rather than lean on either side --- and
gemma4:e2b's constrained arm trailed its
native baseline outright ($-10.5$\,pp). Decisively, the mechanism
check --- the criterion's least method-sensitive clause --- did
not verify on this first pass --- while the envelope parser never
counts as a rescue (by design), argument-level schema failures remained
substantial, because the envelope's \texttt{arguments} field was left
generically typed rather than constrained per-tool.

That gap was exactly the single pre-declared iteration candidate, so we
ran it: replacing the generic \texttt{arguments} schema with a
\texttt{oneOf} over one variant per tool, each pinning \texttt{name} to
that tool and \texttt{arguments} to its real input schema. The
mechanism now verifies cleanly --- argument-level schema failures drop
from 99 to 0 on llama3.2:1b under the same sample --- but pass rate does
not recover; it drops further, on every executor we re-measured
(llama3.2:1b: $-13.7$\,pp relative to the pre-iteration constrained arm,
95\% Newcombe [$-25.3$, $-1.6$]; gemma4:e2b: $-17.9$\,pp [$-30.7$,
$-4.1$]; the qwen2.5:3b control: $-17.9$\,pp [$-30.4$, $-4.5$], on top of
an already-negative format tax). Task-level detail on llama3.2:1b makes
the mechanism concrete: \texttt{glob\_basic} and \texttt{grep\_basic}
passed 100\% of the time under the loose schema and 0\% under the
tightened one, with the losses landing as tool-execution failures, not
schema failures --- committing the decoder to the exact shape of one
specific tool's real arguments in a single constrained pass produces
syntactically perfect but behaviorally worse calls. Eliminating the
syntactic failure mode did not recover the correctness it was diagnosed
against; if anything, the stricter constraint made outcomes worse. The
pre-registered criterion's single permitted iteration is now spent, and
the verdict is unambiguous: \textbf{reject}. Syntax-level decoder
control does not pay off for these executors at either schema looseness
we tested --- the harness-side rescue ladder remains the tradeoff that
does not require decoder access, and having decoder access, even used
more precisely, did not settle the comparison in its favor.

\paragraph{An independent in-process replication.} The result above
could still be an artifact of its mechanism: Ollama's structured-output
constraint is server-side, all-or-nothing (the whole completion is
forced into the envelope schema), and outside our control. After this
paper's main evidence was collected we built the opposite
implementation --- an in-process backend linking \texttt{llama.cpp}
directly, where a GBNF grammar masks logits at the \emph{sampler} and a
manually-lazy activation engages the constraint exactly when a tool
call begins (a literal trigger in the token stream) and releases it
when the call's envelope completes, leaving all surrounding text
unconstrained. We tested both an envelope-level grammar (valid JSON,
tool name restricted to the turn's real inventory, closing tag
guaranteed) and a stricter per-tool grammar derived from each tool's
input schema (required fields forced, in declared order; undeclared
arguments unproducible), with the hypothesis declared in-repo before
each run (git-only registry, \S\ref{sec:setup}). Across three paired
sweeps on \texttt{qwen2.5:3b} (19 tasks $\times$ 3 repetitions per arm,
greedy decoding, exact McNemar on task-repetition pairs), pass rate was
statistically indistinguishable every time (41/57 vs.\ 40/57, 41 vs.\
40, 40 vs.\ 40; $p{=}1.0$ in each) --- and, unlike the server-side
variant, with \emph{no} capability tax: constraining cost nothing. The
mechanism explanation is the more instructive half: the engine's own
downstream repair loop (schema-validation failures are returned to the
model with a corrective message and retried) already absorbs the
residual failure class --- in the final sweep, \emph{every} run that
recorded a schema-validation failure still passed, in both arms. The
syntactic guarantee is real and free, but on this suite there is no
failure class left for it to kill: harness-level recovery downstream
had already covered it. Two implementations at opposite ends of the
decoder-access spectrum thus converge on the same conclusion for
different reasons --- one hurt, one was neutral; neither paid.

\paragraph{A methodological yield.} The in-process replication also
demonstrates why we insist on live verification over test suites
(\S\ref{sec:threats}): wiring the grammar into the sampler surfaced
three latent decoder-integration bugs --- a double-accept advancing the
grammar automaton twice per token (harmless under stateless greedy
sampling, fatal under any stateful sampler), a prompt exceeding the
decode batch size aborting the whole process from C++, and an
unrenderable control token killing the completion stream --- none of
which the workspace's $\sim$1{,}000 unit tests or prior smoke tests had
exposed, and one of which asymmetrically contaminated the first paired
sweep (its three baseline-arm failures were backend crashes, not model
errors) and forced the re-run we report. Constrained decoding did not
improve the model's reliability, but \emph{measuring} it improved the
harness's.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Not all scaffolding helps, and the sign depends on what kind
of lever it is.} Across every result in \S\ref{sec:results}, levers
that add \emph{capability} early in a turn --- a stronger model opening
the first rounds, decoder-level syntax control for the weakest
executor --- either help or are neutral; levers that add \emph{context}
--- a plan, a tool inventory --- stop harming, and at some scales
help, only when delivered in a format that survives the trip through
the serving layer, and the failure when it does not is
scale-independent. A plan injected in the assistant role is not read
as guidance by any executor we tested, small or large, because the
chat template renders it as a turn to continue rather than a question
to answer (\S\ref{sec:planner}); re-delivered as user-role context, or
as re-injected structured state, the same content stops doing damage.
Naively, one might expect "more context helps more capable models
less" as a smooth function of scale; what we observe instead is a
delivery-format cliff that has nothing to do with scale on one side of
it and ordinary diminishing returns on the other. This is why the
harness-vs-scale curve of \S\ref{sec:curve} is not one curve but two,
moving in opposite directions from the same design intention --- and
why the more useful lesson for a harness author is to check what the
serving layer does with each role before attributing a failure to the
model.

\paragraph{A harness for small models needs to make its constraints
legible, not just enforce them.} \S\ref{sec:mechanism}'s reactive
escalation trigger and \S\ref{sec:ablations}'s per-family textual
rescue both fail the same way: not by being wrong, but by having
narrower coverage than the failure surface actually observed, silently,
until someone looks. A harness note announcing an approaching turn
budget or iteration cap converts an abrupt, silent cutoff into an
acknowledged deadline the model can react to before running out of
room --- we ship this lever (Table~\ref{tab:levers}) on the same
reasoning, though we have not yet isolated its own contribution with a
dedicated ablation. The general principle these findings point to is
that a small model benefits from a harness that tells it what regime it
is in --- a budget, a role, a structured plan --- rather than one that
assumes it will infer that regime correctly from context alone; the
inference itself is where small models are least reliable.

\paragraph{Comprehension and faithful emission are different
capabilities, and an agentic loop depends on the second.} A failure
mode we did not anticipate, and which none of our gates catches, is
that a model can read, reason about, and act on a character it cannot
reproduce. Exercising \texttt{gpt-oss:20b} on repository-level
refactors outside our suite (\texttt{docs/roam-metrics-memoria-2026-07-28.md}),
the subscript \texttt{U+1D62} in a doc-comment formula was silently
dropped in two independent copy operations two days apart --- a
216-line block and an 89-line one --- each time leaving a
mathematically false formula that compiles, passes the project's
tests, and passes our own post-edit \texttt{cargo check} guardrail,
because a doc comment is invisible to all three by construction. The
second-order consequence is worse than the corruption. Since
\texttt{edit\_file} matches on exact text, a region containing such a
character becomes \emph{structurally uneditable} by the agent: asked to
delete that block, with its exact text supplied verbatim in the prompt,
the model produced a 91-line \texttt{old\_string} correct on every line
but that one, then spent six attempts, the turn's full twenty-round
budget and twenty-five minutes alternating between the unmatchable call
and re-reading the file, never recovering the cause --- the failure
signal (\texttt{old\_string not found}) carries no information about
\emph{which} character is wrong. On the next module the same
\texttt{edit\_file} failure drove the model to fall back to rewriting
the whole file, which was worse still: the 268-line rewrite converted
two tolerance-based float assertions into exact equalities, deleted
\texttt{U+2248} from three derivation comments, and dropped the
module-link edit it had been asked to make. Only the last of those is
loud --- a missing \texttt{mod} declaration makes the test target fail
to compile --- and reconstructing the rewritten file from the session
log confirms that with that one repaired, the other two pass the
project's suite 14/14. The gate that forced discovery was incidental to
the regressions that mattered.

We report this as a case observation rather than a measured rate: one
model, one repository, no controlled sweep. But it cuts against a
direction recent work points toward. Zhu et al.'s probe of whether
LLM-to-LLM channels need human-readable text at all
\citep{zhu2026babeltele} finds that instruction-tuned models can
\emph{interpret} compressed, symbol- and emoji-dense, multilingual
encodings whose human comprehension collapses to near chance
(35.8\,\% versus 56.1\,\% on four-option QA against a 25\,\% floor),
and reads this as licence to optimise intermediate representations for
model decodability instead of readability. Their evidence concerns
interpretation; ours concerns the dual capability. In a tool-calling
loop the model must \emph{emit} exact strings --- paths, identifiers,
an \texttt{old\_string} --- and emission fidelity is where we observe
failure, on precisely the symbol classes such an encoding is built
from, silently, and with an error message that does not name its
cause. For an agent-computer interface the operative question is
therefore not whether a representation is readable, but whether the
model that has to reproduce it can. This also bounds the
diff-review discipline we rely on elsewhere (\S\ref{sec:threats}): a
human reading the diff is the only gate that caught any of the
corruptions above, and it is exactly the gate a
readability-relaxed representation would remove.

This episode is also where the two practitioner positions of
\S\ref{sec:related} meet, and neither survives intact. The objection is
right that no harness creates a capability the model lacks: we named
the offending character, showed it on both sides, and the model
reproduced the same omission on its next attempt. The curriculum's
prescription --- when something fails, check the harness before
swapping the model --- is right often enough to teach, but here it
pointed the wrong way for twenty-five minutes, because a capability gap
and a harness defect present identically at the tool boundary
(\texttt{old\_string not found} reads as a broken matcher). What the
harness could do is narrower than the curriculum implies and larger
than the objection allows: not repair the failure, but classify it,
report the codepoint, and convert a blind twenty-round deadlock into an
explicit refusal in four with no collateral damage. We take that as the
operative form of the prescription: a harness for small models should
be built to tell its operator which of the two it is in, because that
is the one thing neither a better model nor a longer retry loop
supplies.

\paragraph{Pre-registration functioned as an engineering safeguard, not
a formality.} Twice in this paper's own history, a criterion committed
before a sweep ran changed what we did with a marginal result: the
planner's initial negative-but-inconclusive A/B (\S\ref{sec:planner})
stayed opt-in with a named iteration path rather than being discarded or
rationalized into a keep, and the constrained-decoding investigation
(\S\ref{sec:constrained}) reported an iteration-eligible negative result
rather than either stretching a positive-but-noisy point estimate into
an adoption or force-fitting it into the rejection bucket its own
criterion did not literally describe. In both cases the discipline cost
us the ability to claim a cleaner story than the data supported --- and
that is exactly the failure mode it exists to prevent when the person
writing the criterion is also the person who built the lever it judges.

\paragraph{Practical implication.} For a practitioner deploying a
small, local model under cost, privacy, or latency constraints, the
honest reading of \S\ref{sec:curve}--\ref{sec:external} is simpler
than a composition recipe: \emph{run the most capable small model you
can serve, alone}. The composite buys no correctness beyond the lead's
own ceiling, and --- measured head-to-head on the three same-commit,
same-day power sweeps --- it does not buy cost either: the 1B+lead
composite averages 2{,}779 input tokens, 342 output tokens, and 23.9\,s
per run against the solo lead's 2{,}910 / 371 / 25.2\,s
($\times$0.92--0.95, i.e.\ within a few percent;
\texttt{docs/emse-r2-analysis-2026-07-17.md}). Nor is the composite
cheap relative to the mid-scale baselines it outperforms: against the
7B baseline it spends roughly $1.2\times$ the input tokens, $5\times$
the output tokens, and $2.5\times$ the wall-clock per run
(cross-sweep, so read as magnitudes). An earlier draft of this paper
claimed the 1B+lead composite wins ``at a fraction of the 7B's
inference cost''; the measured token and latency data do not support
that claim and we withdraw it. Composing a lead with a smaller
executor is left as the right tool for regimes this paper did not
measure --- a lead that cannot serve whole turns (context length,
per-token pricing asymmetry, a lead shared across many executors), or
memory budgets that fit 1B+4B but not a single larger model. The
context-side levers we measured are the opposite of low-risk without
matching their delivery to the executor's scale, and the deferral and
constrained-decoding levers of
\S\ref{sec:searchtools}--\ref{sec:constrained} are genuinely conditional:
worth adopting only once an inventory does not fit context, or only for
the specific weak-executor regime where their measured effect, however
noisy, points in the helpful direction.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Suite coverage (external and construct validity).} Most
results are measured on one 19-task suite we authored, spanning five
skills chosen to separate common agentic failure modes (tool
selection, multi-step composition, error recovery, distractor
rejection) rather than to match any published benchmark's task
distribution --- purposive construction, not sampling from any defined
task population, with the generalization limits that entails
\citep{baltes2022sampling}. The sharpest version of this threat is circularity: the
same 19 tasks served as the development-time regression target for the
harness whose levers this paper evaluates --- several hardening passes
tuned assertions and defaults against failures on exactly this suite
--- so levers may be fit to the suite in ways an external task set
would expose. Two pre-registered anchors now bound this threat for
the paper's \emph{central} claims: on a BFCL subset the lead lever's
effect at 1B ($+72.0$\,pp), the baseline ordering, and the
ceiling-pinning null all replicate (\S\ref{sec:bfcl}); and on an
execution-graded SWE-bench Lite slice the lever's behavioral effect
transfers outside noise and the pinned ceiling holds, while the
resolution-level effect is directional only at $n{=}40$ per arm
(\S\ref{sec:swebench}) --- which is itself the bound: nothing in this
paper shows a reliability lever conferring repository-level task
competence a model lacks. The threat is not eliminated for the rest:
the planner/delivery results, the tool-deferral cost,
and the secondary ablations remain measured on the authored suite
only; the BFCL anchor covers three single-turn categories, and the
SWE-bench slice is 20 instances at 2 repetitions --- coverage is
partial by design in both. Relatedly, the authored suite leaves
little headroom at the top of the
scale range: with the solo lead at 91\% and the ceiling baseline at
98\%, a composite has at most a few points of room in which to
demonstrate a gain over the lead alone, so the three-way null of
\S\ref{sec:external} is partially built into the instrument --- tasks
this saturated cannot, by construction, exhibit large composition
gains; a harder suite could (though the anchor's own version of the
null, at a 92.7\% ceiling, repeats the pattern).

\paragraph{Tool-identity grading is not neutral across model families
(construct validity).} \S\ref{sec:bfcl}'s two grading levels disagree
in opposite directions depending on the model: the identity proxy
\emph{overstates} the 1B executor by 47\,pp (right tool, wrong
arguments) and \emph{understates} the thinking model by 9\,pp
(correct calls the proxy fails to credit; we report the direction
only --- we did not diagnose the proxy's precise failure mode on
this model's long, multi-round transcripts). Any evaluation that
grades agentic tool use by ``did it call the expected tool'' ---
including \braze{}'s own live runner metric, and informal
comparisons between published numbers graded at different levels ---
inherits a family-dependent bias, not just added noise. The authored
suite's semantic outcome checks (needle assertions, compile-checked
edits) sit closer to the AST level than to the identity level, which
is why the authored and offline-anchor numbers agree as well as they
do in \S\ref{sec:bfcl}'s E1.

\paragraph{The external comparison point is independent in orchestration
logic only, not in underlying infrastructure (construct validity).} \S\ref{sec:external}'s
bare lead+executor loop was planned as a scale-curve anchor early in
this project and, unlike that early stub, was implemented and run: it
reimplements the turn loop, the lead/executor split, and the tool-call
handling from scratch rather than reusing \texttt{braze\_engine::Engine}
or \texttt{EscalatingBackend}. It still calls Ollama through
\texttt{braze\_model}'s HTTP client and executes tools through
\texttt{braze\_tools\_local}'s implementations --- the same
infrastructure every other row in this paper uses --- so it isolates
\braze{}'s \emph{orchestration engineering} (the levers in
Table~\ref{tab:levers}) from the composition pattern, not from every
line of code in the workspace. A comparison against a fully independent
third-party harness (candidate identified but not implemented,
\citep{pi-dev}) remains the stronger version of this test. Separately,
this arm was run at one executor scale (1B) rather than across the
full curve, for the same compute-budget reasons \S\ref{sec:setup}
already documents for other secondary levers.

\paragraph{Single fixed helper model (external validity).} Every lead and planner role in
every sweep is filled by the same model, gemma4:e4b. We closed part of
this threat under pre-registration (\texttt{
docs/gemma4-e4b-solo-baseline-design.md}): gemma4:e4b's own solo pass
rate on this suite (91\% pooled at $n{=}285$, \S\ref{sec:curve}) is
statistically indistinguishable from the 1B+lead composite it anchors, which
resolves \emph{whether} the curve's headline number is sensitive to
this choice --- it is, in the specific sense that the composite's
capability ceiling is largely gemma4:e4b's own ceiling, not an emergent
property of the composition. What remains open is whether a
\emph{different} lead model, at a different capability level relative
to its executor, would reproduce the same qualitative pattern (gain
decaying to zero as the executor approaches the lead's own capability)
or would instead reveal genuine executor-side synergy that gemma4:e4b's
specific capability happened to mask; an earlier, narrower comparison
(not reported in detail here) found a qualitatively similar
cost/latency tradeoff between two candidate lead models at roughly
$2\times$ latency difference, consistent with the choice of gemma4:e4b
not being arbitrary, but this is not a systematic sensitivity analysis
across lead-model capability levels.

\paragraph{Sample size at the skill level (conclusion validity).} Aggregate pass rates use
$n{=}95$ per cell (19 tasks $\times$ 5 repetitions), but per-skill
breakdowns are $n{=}15$: enough to distinguish the extremes we report
(0/15 vs.\ 14/15) but not enough to resolve the smaller differences we
also report (e.g.\ 13 vs.\ 14 vs.\ 14 on \texttt{distractor\_selection}
in \S\ref{sec:mechanism}) from noise; we flag these as directional
where we cite them, not as independently significant.

\paragraph{Model family coverage (external validity).} The executor scale range is
dominated by one model family (qwen2.5:3b/7b and qwen3.5-coder are all
Qwen) plus one non-function-calling-tuned generic model
(llama3.2:1b); the constrained-decoding investigation adds gemma4:e2b
and gemma3:1b, but no result in this paper spans more than two model
families at once. Effects attributed to "scale" may be partially
confounded with Qwen-specific training choices --- particularly the
tool-template overfitting effects \S\ref{sec:related} documents in the
literature for this family specifically.

\paragraph{Measurement artifacts in already-run sweeps, disclosed (internal validity).} A
harness-hardening pass after several of this paper's sweeps had already
run fixed measurement issues that could otherwise bias the numbers we
report; where a fix postdates a cited sweep, we disclose the direction
of the bias rather than silently re-run everything. A timed-out run's
token and round counts are a lower bound, not an exact count --- the
in-flight round is lost when the attempt is cut off --- so a cost
comparison between arms with different timeout rates favors whichever
arm times out more, an effect relevant to \S\ref{sec:searchtools}'s
latency numbers if not to its pass rates. The tool-deferral arm's
context budget (\S\ref{sec:searchtools}) was computed against the
\emph{full} pre-deferral tool catalog rather than the smaller,
already-deferred prompt the model actually saw, which made that arm
compact more eagerly than necessary --- a bias against the deferral
arm, meaning the reported $5$--$6\times$ token savings are conservative,
not inflated. Sweeps that predate a later warm-up fix could pay a
model's cold-load time on the first task of the first arm under
\texttt{--no-ollama-stop}, which risks an inflated wall-time (and, on
CPU, a spurious timeout) concentrated on that one cell; this does not
affect the pass rates we report except through that timeout channel.
One result was fully re-verified rather than merely disclosed: an
earlier measurement of the composed planner-and-lead arm
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}) turned out to have a task-list summary that
silently reset the lead's reactive-escalation failure streak, making
reactive escalation structurally unable to fire in that arm; a
controlled re-run with the fix in place (190 runs, including an anchor
cell) reproduced the same qualitative verdict with the mechanism now
firing for real (9 genuine escalations), which we report as a
confirmation, not merely an assumption the original result still holds.
Finally, one text-match assertion used across earlier sweeps accepted a
model's pre-tool-call narration anywhere in a turn's text as satisfying
a content check, which plausibly favored more verbose models in cells
we do not re-cite at the skill level in this paper; \S\ref{sec:searchtools}'s
headline numbers use the corrected, stricter assertion specifically
because this issue was live when its first, retracted null result was
measured.

\paragraph{Infrastructure failures, disclosed rather than silently
excluded (internal validity).} Two sweeps in this paper encountered transient Nitro
infrastructure failures during data collection. One arm of the planner
A/B (\S\ref{sec:planner}, the 3B task-list delivery) had 58 of 62
failures on its first attempt be transient network errors concentrated
entirely in that arm; we re-ran it cleanly and report the clean re-run,
disclosing the contaminated attempt rather than silently substituting
it. A later audit prompted by this paper's second pre-submission
review found the same event also reached that sweep's coder arms
asymmetrically (10 transport failures on the baseline, 2 on the
user-role arm, 8 on the task-list arm), depressing the coder baseline
and inflating the apparent gain of the fixed deliveries. An
intermediate revision corrected this with an analytical exclusion
(criterion and raw-vs-corrected numbers in
\texttt{docs/emse-r2-analysis-2026-07-17.md}); \S\ref{sec:planner}
now reports a pre-registered clean re-measurement of all three coder
arms instead --- whose own first attempt was invalidated by the
committed 2\%-transport rule after exposing a circuit-breaker
probe-cancellation bug in the harness (fixed with regression tests;
attempt preserved with a \texttt{.contaminated} suffix), and whose
valid attempt has zero transport failures. Three generations of the
same numbers --- contaminated, exclusion-corrected, cleanly
re-measured --- are all preserved in the repository. The scale-curve sweep (\S\ref{sec:curve}) was collected in two
slices roughly eight hours apart after Nitro became unreachable
mid-sweep; the interrupted slice's 1{,}140 dead rows were excluded from
analysis, and the two valid slices are treated as coming from the same
binary and configuration but not necessarily the same thermal/load
session on the inference node, so cross-slice latency comparisons are
read as orders of magnitude rather than precise deltas.

A third audit --- this one prompted by an unrelated infrastructure
incident months after collection, and applying the criterion above
unchanged to a sweep it was not designed for --- found that the same
failure mode had reached the scale-curve sweep itself, concentrated in
one cell (\texttt{docs/curve-transport-audit-2026-07-18.md}). The
composed planner-and-lead arm at 1B lost 30 of 95 runs to transport
failures, which moved that cell from 89.2\% to a reported 61.1\% and,
with it, the interpretation of the composition at the smallest scale
(\S\ref{sec:curve} reports the correction, its provenance, and the
pre-registered re-run that will supersede it). The remaining fifteen
cells of that sweep carry between zero and four such runs: applying the
same exclusion would move \texttt{qwen2.5:3b}'s baseline by
$+3.0$\,pp, \texttt{qwen3.5-coder}'s by $+2.1$\,pp,
\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}'s by $+0.9$\,pp and \texttt{qwen3.5-coder+lead}'s
by $+1.0$\,pp, each well inside its own Wilson interval and changing no
comparison in this paper; the other eleven cells are unaffected. We
report those magnitudes rather than restate the cells, because
excluding one to four runs from cells whose intervals span ten points
is bookkeeping, while excluding a third of a cell that scored zero on
the excluded portion is not. Both readings are recoverable from the
committed raw data either way. The broader lesson we take from three
separate audits finding the same failure mode is that a transport
error and a model failure are easy to conflate at analysis time unless
the harness distinguishes them at collection time; the retry and
logging path added afterwards
(\texttt{BRAZE\_OLLAMA\_TRANSPORT\_RETRIES}) exists for that reason.

\paragraph{Multiplicity (conclusion validity).} This paper reports on
the order of twenty between-arm interval comparisons with no
family-wise correction (\S\ref{sec:setup}). The pre-registered
criteria bound researcher degrees of freedom on the adopt/reject
decisions they govern, but any individual interval cited outside those
criteria --- including the per-skill contrasts --- should be read as
uncorrected. A related asymmetry postdates most of the evidence: the
harness's benchmark reporter now computes exact paired McNemar tests
with Holm correction against a designated baseline arm (and the
$\text{pass}^k$ reliability statistic of $\tau$-bench), and the
in-process constrained-decoding replication (\S\ref{sec:constrained})
uses that paired machinery; the earlier sweeps in
Table~\ref{tab:sweeps} were compared with unpaired
Newcombe intervals on independent proportions, which is conservative
for the shared-task design (tasks are identical across arms) --- a
paired re-analysis of the archived JSONs is possible from the released
artifacts and would, if anything, narrow those intervals.

\paragraph{Mechanism claims (construct validity).} Two mechanism
claims rest on probes of a single harness-and-serving stack. The
prefill account of the planner's empty-response degeneration
(\S\ref{sec:planner}) now rests on documentary evidence --- the served
chat templates of two of the three affected executors state the rule
outright --- plus a pre-registered behavioural probe that confirms the
mechanism for \texttt{llama3.2:1b} (immediate end-of-turn,
\texttt{eval\_count}~$=1$, in 10/10 assistant-last requests) but
\emph{not} for \texttt{qwen3.5-coder}, whose collapse the bare role
manipulation does not reproduce. Two incompletenesses remain: the
templates of the coder, ceiling, and helper models are rendered by
built-in renderers we cannot inspect --- and the coder's probe
divergence makes that opacity a live limitation, not a formality ---
and the token accounting of full-prompt empty turns (47--594 generated
tokens in the sweep, versus one token in the minimal probe) is
unclosed: the mechanism claim is scoped to the executor and setting
where it verified. Compositional brittleness
(\S\ref{sec:searchtools}) rests on one 45-run matched-seed probe on
one model, corroborated --- not independently established --- by a
5-transcript sample.

\paragraph{Levers are measured individually, not in combination
(construct validity).} \braze{}'s design switches each reliability
lever independently per benchmark row, and this paper reports
single-lever ablations plus four named configurations. Work on the same
harness after these sweeps found two cases in which levers that are
individually correct --- each with passing unit tests, each with a
defensible ablation --- compose into a failure neither exhibits alone.
The sharpest is between the tactical observation-collapse window and
the repeated-call guard: the collapse elides an older tool result to a
single line, and the guard then answers a re-request for that same call
with a refusal (``you already called this; use the result you have''),
so the model is denied content the harness itself removed. Observed
live on a repository-level task, a 20B executor spent four consecutive
calls on that exchange and abandoned the turn with a correct plan in
hand. Nothing here invalidates a measured row --- each row is a fully
specified configuration, and the interaction was not active in the
cells we report --- but it bounds extrapolation: a single-lever
ablation licenses no prediction about an unmeasured combination, and
the additive reading that ablation tables invite is not established. A
factorial over lever \emph{pairs}, with the interaction term as the
estimand, is the instrument this question needs and the one we did not
run.

\paragraph{A configured guardrail can be silently inactive (internal
validity).} The post-edit check (\S\ref{sec:harness}) runs an external
command --- \texttt{cargo check} by default --- and its failure posture
is deliberately quiet: a missing binary, a timeout, or an unrunnable
command all skip without comment, so that the guardrail never turns a
successful edit into a failed tool call. The measurement consequence is
that in an environment where the configured command is not on the
path, the lever is inactive while the benchmark row still reports it as
enabled. We encountered this directly after the sweeps reported here:
a benchmark invocation from a non-interactive shell without the Rust
toolchain on \texttt{PATH} disabled both the guardrail and the
compile-based grading, yielding an all-fail sweep whose signature was
indistinguishable from model incapacity. The ablation rows involving
this lever (\S\ref{sec:ablations}) should be read with that
possibility in view; we have not re-verified the environment of every
archived sweep. The corresponding harness fix is a one-time startup
warning when a configured formatter is not executable, so that a
degraded configuration announces itself rather than presenting as a
result.

\paragraph{Outcome grading does not verify artifact fidelity
(construct validity).} Needle assertions and compile checks establish
that an edit yields a compiling artifact containing the expected
substrings; they do not establish that regions the task did not target
were preserved. On a repository-level refactor we observed a 20B model
silently corrupt content while copying code it had been asked to move
verbatim. One corruption broke syntax and was caught --- by the
compiler, two tasks and roughly thirty minutes later, because the file
was not yet reachable from any module declaration and so had never
been compiled. The second removed subscript characters from a
documentation formula, leaving it mathematically wrong in a file that
compiles, passes every test, and passes the post-edit check. Grading
at the outcome level is therefore structurally blind to a class of
damage confined to comments, string literals and documentation. This
does not affect the pass rates reported here, which concern task
completion rather than artifact fidelity, but it bounds what a passing
run certifies: that the task's stated goal was met, not that nothing
else changed.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The harness-vs-scale curve this paper set out to measure exists, has
mechanisms with identified --- and explicitly bounded --- candidates,
and its exceptions have diagnoses rather than asterisks: a capability
lever lifts the smallest executor we tested by 70 percentage points by
pinning the composite at the lead model's own ceiling, so its apparent
gain decays as executor baselines rise toward that ceiling; a context
lever that
looked like a settled negative result under one delivery format turned
out, once its delivery rather than its content was fixed under
pre-registration, to be a repaired harm at both scale extremes ---
though its committed adoption criterion, applied literally, remains
unmet; and a cost-saving lever with an
apparently free correctness trade-off turned out, at a larger sample
and a hardened harness, to have a real and mechanistically diagnosed
price. A pre-registered solo baseline of the dominant lever's own
helper model tempers the strongest reading of the first result: the
1B-executor-beats-7B-baseline headline is largely attributable to the
lead model's own capability, routed efficiently, rather than to an
emergent property of the composition itself (\S\ref{sec:curve}) --- a
finding we report because our own pre-registered criterion, applied to
our own paper's headline number, told us to. A from-scratch external
comparison point reinforces the same revision from a different angle
(\S\ref{sec:external}): a bare lead+executor loop with none of
\braze{}'s engineering lands within noise of both the full composite and
the helper model alone, the paper's first result measured against
something other than \braze{} itself. The throughline across this
paper's results survives that revision in a narrower but still
substantive form: the harness decides \emph{which} capability in its
ensemble
reaches the task and in what format, and getting that routing right,
measured rather than assumed, is what moves pass rate --- at the sample
sizes this paper measures, we find no evidence that \braze{}'s specific
engineering manufactures capability beyond what its most capable
component and the lead/executor pattern already buy on their own, and
we report that as a finding rather than understate it.

\section{Pre-registration texts}\label{app:prereg}

This paper's discipline of writing an adoption/rejection criterion
before the sweep that decides it is not merely described in
\S\ref{sec:setup} but auditable: each criterion is a committed document
predating its sweep's raw results file. The three texts below are
transcribed verbatim (original language, Spanish, as actually
committed) from the project's internal design log, each with the git
commit that introduced it; an English gloss follows each quote for the
non-Spanish-reading reviewer. All three commits are resolvable in the
project's public history (\S\ref{sec:setup}, ``Artifact availability'').

\paragraph{(1) The planner's adoption/rejection criterion.} Committed
\texttt{dc65e10} (2026-07-06), \emph{PLAN.md} \S~``Split
planificador/ejecutor'', ahead of the \texttt{28f7a53} sweep
\S\ref{sec:planner} reports on:

\begin{quote}\itshape\small
Criterio (pre-comprometido): con \texttt{--seed}/\texttt{--temperature}
fijos y $\geq$5 repeticiones, la variante planificada debe mejorar el
pass rate de \texttt{multi\_step} + \texttt{error\_recovery} fuera del
intervalo de Wilson del baseline (no dentro del ruido de $\pm$5pp), sin
degradar \texttt{no\_tool}/\texttt{single\_tool}/\texttt{distractor\_%
selection}. El costo extra de tokens del planner queda registrado por
las sumas de \texttt{Usage} y entra al veredicto. Si no cumple: t\'ecnica
rechazada, se documenta y se remueve.
\end{quote}

\noindent\emph{Gloss}: with seed/temperature fixed and $\geq$5 reps,
the planned variant must move \texttt{multi\_step}+\texttt{error\_%
recovery} pass rate outside the baseline's Wilson interval (not within
$\pm$5pp noise), without degrading the other three skills; the
planner's own token cost is logged and counts toward the verdict;
failing the bar means rejection and removal.

\paragraph{(2) The isolated-explorer lever (I.7) --- reported here only
as a worked example of the same discipline applied to a lever this
paper does not itself report results on.} Committed \texttt{4517b6d},
\emph{docs/explorador-aislado-ab-design.md} \S~``Criterio
pre-registrado'':

\begin{quote}\itshape\small
Adoptar (promover a implementaci\'on completa + considerar en Fase 2) si
\texttt{+explore} mejora pass rate en $\geq$8pp agregado sobre baseline
en 3b (fuera del ruido con n=30/brazo en las 5 tareas no-control), O si
iguala pass rate con $\geq$30\% menos tokens de turno principal (medido
por \texttt{expect\_max\_tokens}/tokens totales incluyendo al hijo).
Rechazar (cerrar I.7, no implementar isolation en Fase 2 por esta v\'ia)
si \texttt{+explore} no mueve ninguna de las dos m\'etricas, o si el
control negativo (tarea 6) muestra delegaci\'on compulsiva ($>$2/5 reps
delegando lo que estaba en el prompt). Iterar UNA vez (misma regla que
el planner) solo si el modo de falla dominante es identificable y
atacable (p.ej. el hijo converge pero el padre ignora su respuesta
$\to$ problema de render del tool result, no de la palanca).
Pre-registrado ahora para no re-litigar despu\'es.
\end{quote}

\noindent\emph{Gloss}: adopt if the isolated-child lever beats baseline
by $\geq$8pp aggregate on the 3B executor, or matches pass rate at
$\geq$30\% fewer main-turn tokens; reject if neither metric moves, or
if the negative control shows compulsive delegation; one permitted
iteration if the dominant failure mode is a fixable rendering bug
rather than the lever itself.

\paragraph{(3) The constrained-decoding criterion.} Committed
\texttt{a279495}, \emph{docs/constrained-decoding-ab-design.md}
\S~``Criterio pre-registrado'' --- this is the same adopt/reject/iterate
language paraphrased against the numbers in \S\ref{sec:constrained}:

\begin{quote}\itshape\small
Adoptar (promover \texttt{constrained-tools} a knob de config
documentado para modelos d\'ebiles, secci\'on en el paper) si, en al menos
un executor d\'ebil (llama3.2:1b o gemma4:e2b): C $-$ A $\geq$ +10pp

\noindent\emph{Gloss}: adopt constrained decoding if it beats both the
native-tools baseline (A) by $\geq$10pp on a weak executor \emph{and}
beats the prompted-schema arm (B) --- isolating the gain to the
decoder-level constraint itself, not to the prompt reformulation the
constrained condition also happens to carry --- with near-zero residual
schema failures; reject if it does not clear baseline, or clears it
only by matching B's gain; one permitted iteration, pre-declared to a
single named failure mode (a generic \texttt{object} schema letting
malformed per-tool arguments through).
