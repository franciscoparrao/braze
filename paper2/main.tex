\begin{abstract}
% Fuente: docs/paper2-honest-outline-2026-08-11.md (§ claim + § por qué
% un nulo es publicable).
Coding agents built on small, locally hosted language models are
attractive for cost, privacy, and autonomy. An equally attractive idea
promises to improve them over time: when the local model fails and a
cloud model rescues it, distill the intervention into a reusable
procedural playbook, so that the escalation is paid once and amortized
across future tasks. We show that this idea, in its natural
prompt-injected form, faces a narrow and quantifiable
\emph{amortization frontier}, and that realistic procedural memory
falls on the wrong side of it precisely where it would be needed. An
agentic loop re-sends its context on every round, so an injected
memory section costs its size times the number of rounds and pays for
itself only if it saves enough rounds. In the configurations we
measure, break-even is one to nearly two full rounds of interaction.
We ran
two independent pre-registered evaluations on a local-first coding
harness with a 20B-parameter mixture-of-experts executor
($\sim$3.6B active parameters; 140 and 306 runs), plus a
pre-registered 140-run replication of the first study on a dense 9B
executor. A human-written playbook, the ceiling for any automatically distilled
variant, amortizes only on the task the model already knows, where it
is redundant. On fresh tasks, where it would matter, it misses
break-even by a factor of $3$--$6$; success rates show no reliable
change anywhere (all intervals include zero). On the smaller executor
the same playbook is outright harmful: it lengthens trajectories on
every task and significantly reduces success on the fresh ones. As
the executor weakens, the failure mode shifts from economic to
behavioral. In the second study, a same-prompt control arm dissolved an
apparently significant $+13$-task gain (McNemar $p=0.011$,
Holm-corrected) into an unattributable $+4$ ($p=0.541$): without that
arm, we would have reported a false positive that met its
pre-registered promotion criterion. The measured noise floor
($\sim$20\% of paired cells flip under byte-identical prompts) is
configuration-specific and an order of magnitude above the floor
imported from an easier suite. The design lesson carries a measured bar rather
than a speculative one: prompt-injected procedural memory pays only
if the injection cost drops (retrieval on demand) or the round
savings rise (interventions that unlock task classes the model fails outright);
both are directions whose break-even this paper quantifies but whose
designs remain future work.
\keywords{agentic coding \and procedural memory \and small language
models \and negative results \and pre-registration \and same-prompt
control}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% Fuente: outline § "El giro" + § "Por qué un nulo es publicable".

Coding agents that run on small, locally hosted language models are
increasingly practical: free to run, private by construction, and fast
enough on commodity hardware to be useful
\citep{lu2024smalllm,corradini2025}. They also fail more often than
their frontier counterparts, which makes one idea for improving them
especially attractive. When the local model fails and a stronger cloud
model rescues it, distill the tutor's intervention into a reusable
procedural playbook (\emph{procedural memory}) and inject it into
future related tasks: the escalation is paid once and amortized
forever after. Variants of this idea circulate widely in the agent
literature as skill libraries, stored reflections, and memory
subsystems (Section~\ref{sec:related}). It sounds obviously good, and
it is exactly the kind of plausible technique that gets adopted
without measurement.

This paper measures it, and the measurement says no --- with a
mechanism. The economics are structural. An agentic loop re-sends the
growing conversation to the model on every round, so a memory section
of $c$ tokens does not cost $c$ tokens per task: it costs $c \times
R$, where $R$ is the number of rounds the turn takes. Its benefit,
meanwhile, arrives only as \emph{rounds saved}. Balancing the two
yields an explicit amortization condition
(Section~\ref{sec:model}): in the configurations measured here, an
injected memory must eliminate roughly one full round of interaction
to break even.

We report two independent, pre-registered evaluations of
prompt-injected procedural memory on \braze{}, a local-first agentic
coding harness, with a 20B-parameter local executor. Study~1
instantiates a multi-session benchmark (session A produces a
playbook, same-family tasks B measure transfer, a holdout H prices
misapplication) with a \emph{human-written} playbook, the ceiling
for any automatically distilled variant, across three compile-repair
tasks (140 runs). Study~2 evaluates the production form of the same
idea, a project-memory hook that injects the files touched by previous
sessions, in a three-arm A/B whose middle arm is a \emph{same-prompt
control}: its prompt is byte-identical to the baseline by construction
(306 runs). A pre-registered replication of Study~1 on a second, dense
9B executor (140 further runs) tests whether the pattern is specific
to one architecture.

Both studies land on the same side of the frontier, though they probe
different points of it: Study~1 prices content-rich memory, Study~2 a
content-thin one. The playbook
amortizes only on the task the model already knows, where it is
redundant, and misses break-even by a factor of $3$--$6$ on fresh
tasks, where it would matter. Success rates show no reliable change
anywhere. In Study~2 the treatment arm beat baseline by $+13$ tasks
(McNemar $p=0.011$, Holm-corrected), formally meeting the
pre-registered promotion criterion. However, the same-prompt control
dissolved the result: the control arm rose $+9$ on an identical
prompt, and the only contrast that isolates the memory's content was
null ($+4$, $p=0.541$). The replication preserves the
anti-correlation's ordering and worsens its regime: on the smaller
executor the playbook lengthens trajectories everywhere and
significantly reduces success on fresh tasks. A collateral finding has independent value:
the noise floor of the configuration is $\sim$20\% of paired cells
under byte-identical prompts, an order of magnitude above the floor
the pre-registration had imported from an easier suite.

This paper contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a formalization of the \emph{amortization condition} for
  prompt-injected procedural memory in agentic loops, whose central
  empirical content is an anti-correlation between when memory helps
  and when it is needed (Sections~\ref{sec:model}
  and~\ref{sec:results});
\item two independent pre-registered null results that confirm it,
  plus a pre-registered replication in which the failure deepens from
  economic to behavioral on a smaller executor, together with the
  \emph{same-prompt control} as a transferable methodological
  instrument; without it, this paper would instead
  report a significant false positive
  (Section~\ref{sec:results-pm});
\item the multi-session benchmark design A$\to$B$\to$H for measuring
  procedural transfer under objective oracles, together with its first
  (partial) instantiation: the pilot exercises sessions B and H
  against a human-written playbook, and the live tutor of session A
  is future work (Section~\ref{sec:method});
\item a design lesson with measured directions rather than
  speculation: injected memory pays only if the injection cost drops
  (retrieval on demand) or the round savings rise (class-unlocking
  interventions) (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% /verify-refs pasa en S4 sobre las entradas nuevas (ver refs.bib).

\paragraph{Memory-augmented LLM agents.} Cross-episode memory is an
established lever in the agent literature. Voyager accumulates an
ever-growing library of executable skills that later episodes retrieve
and compose \citep{wang2023voyager}; Reflexion stores verbal
self-reflections from failed trials and conditions subsequent attempts
on them \citep{shinn2023reflexion}; MemGPT manages the context window
as virtual memory, paging information between the prompt and external
storage \citep{packer2023memgpt}. These systems established that
memory \emph{can} improve agents, in evaluations centered on
frontier-scale models and success metrics. What carrying the memory
costs (on every round, in a loop that re-sends its context) is
generally not priced, and the effect of the memory's \emph{content} is
rarely separated from the effect of perturbing the prompt at all. Both
gaps are the subject of this paper.

\paragraph{Concurrent work: evolved skills at larger scale.} While
this paper was under preparation, \citet{tang2026wikiskill} introduced
WikiSkill, which co-evolves agent skills with a persistent wiki and
reports large accuracy gains over no-skill baselines across five
benchmarks and five models. Read against our result the two are
complementary rather than contradictory, for three reasons. First, the
endpoints differ: WikiSkill measures accuracy and does not price the
context its skills occupy, even though those skills are injected in
full into the system prompt on every round and average between 45 and
143 markdown lines depending on model and benchmark --- precisely the
per-round tax whose break-even Section~\ref{sec:model} quantifies.
Second, the mechanism differs: their skills are evolved across
iterations under a validation gate with rollback, whereas ours is a
fixed human-written playbook, adopted as the ceiling for automatically
distilled variants rather than as a competing optimizer. Third, and
most relevant to scope, their gains grow monotonically with executor
capability ($+12.3$, $+17.5$ and $+23.9$ points at 4B, 9B and 27B);
our executor sits below the low end of that range at $\sim$3.6B active
parameters, so their own gradient predicts our regime to be the least
favourable one for injected procedural memory. Their design assumes
the injection side is affordable; this paper measures its price.

\paragraph{Small and local models as executors.} Surveys of small
language models document the capability and deployment trade-offs of
the sub-10B regime \citep{lu2024smalllm,corradini2025}; our executor,
a 20B mixture-of-experts with $\sim$3.6B active parameters, runs on
commodity hardware at the practical edge of that regime. Two
properties of this regime sharpen the memory question. First, under local
inference the input-token bill is paid in wall-clock time on the
user's hardware on every round, so per-round context overhead is a
first-order design cost, not a billing detail. Second, small executors
are sensitive to context additions in ways that are hard to predict:
the companion study to this paper measured harness levers one at a
time at small-model scales and found ``obviously good'' context
additions frequently null or harmful \citep{parra2026scaffolding}; that result
is the prior behind the honest expectations pre-registered here.
Compressing injected context is a complementary lever: models can
consume text that is no longer human-readable \citep{zhu2026babeltele}.
That lowers the per-round tax but leaves the amortization question
intact.

\paragraph{The harness as an engineering object.} SWE-agent showed
that the agent-computer interface changes outcomes at fixed model
\citep{yang2024sweagent}; practitioner curricula and post-mortems now
treat ``harness engineering'' as a named discipline
\citep{walkinglabs2026,dex2026wsff}, and LoopsBench extends the framing
from the harness to the whole evaluation loop \citep{li2026loopsbench}.
Practitioner-facing empirical work has begun to measure this layer
directly: interview studies find that configuring and steering agentic
frameworks is itself a recurring pain point for the developers who
operate them \citep{hamid2026hands}, and cost--benefit studies of
assistant output ask how many candidate solutions are worth inspecting
before returns diminish \citep{oertel2025copilot}; this paper takes
the same measurement-first stance toward injected memory.
Two recent results make the memory component specifically urgent.
CompInt quantifies silent \emph{loss}: user-issued side constraints
survive context compaction only a minority of the time, and the
authors close the gap with an extraction module separate from the
compactor rather than with better compaction prompts
\citep{wang2026compint}. AutoDesign formalizes the harness as a
recursive optimization target (a meta-harness optimizer proposes one
bounded update per iteration to one of five components, of which
Context \& Memory is one), accepting updates when a train score
improves and a dev score does not regress \citep{luo2026autodesign}.
Neither line prices the \emph{injection} side of memory.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} argues that the amortization condition of
Section~\ref{sec:model} is the acceptance criterion that side needs,
and that in the small-model noise regime a point-estimate acceptance
gate would have promoted the false positive our control arm caught.

\paragraph{Negative results and pre-registration in empirical software
engineering.} Pre-registration separates hypothesis generation from
confirmation \citep{nosek2018preregistration}, and open-science
initiatives in empirical software engineering journals have
institutionalized the transparency and reproducibility expectations
that make a well-documented null
publishable \citep{mendez2019openscience}; community standards make
analogous demands on benchmark-based studies \citep{ralph2021standards},
and the statistical treatment of randomized SE experiments is
well-codified \citep{arcuri2011practical}. This paper follows that
discipline in a lightweight form: both studies were registered by
committing hypotheses, decision criteria, and an honest prior to the
project repository before launch; Study~2's confirmatory contrasts
are paired exact tests with multiplicity correction, and Study~1's
estimates carry confidence intervals with Holm-corrected tests per
endpoint family; the headline result is a null reported as such.

\section{The Amortization Condition}\label{sec:model}
% Fuente: outline § "El claim que los datos SÍ sostienen". Sección
% CENTRAL y corta — la fórmula que Results instancia.

Prompt-injected procedural memory (a playbook, a list of files
touched in previous sessions, a distilled tutor intervention) enters
the agent's context on \emph{every} round of a turn: agentic
loops re-send the (growing) conversation to the model each round, so a
memory section of $c$ tokens does not cost $c$ tokens per task but
$c \times R$ tokens, where $R$ is the number of rounds the turn takes.
Its benefit, in turn, is realized as \emph{rounds saved}: if the
memory actually guides the model, the turn converges earlier.

Two clarifications about what that cost is, and is not. Modern
serving stacks cache the key--value state of a stable prefix, so an
injected section is \emph{computed} once rather than $R$ times; the
$c \times R$ term is therefore an accounting and occupancy cost, not
a recomputation cost. It is nonetheless real on three counts: hosted
APIs meter input tokens per round (discounted under explicit prompt
caching, not free); the cached section occupies key--value memory for
the whole turn, competing with the rest of the context, which is
material on constrained hardware; and carrying the section changes
what the model sees on every round, so its effect on the trajectory
is behavioral, not merely fiscal. Our own measurements separate these
components: on the two fresh tasks of Section~\ref{sec:results-m1}
the playbook adds ${\sim}1{,}100$ net input tokens with \emph{no}
reliable change in wall time (Table~\ref{tab:m1}), which is the
signature of a cached prefix --- while on the weaker executor of
Section~\ref{sec:results-ornith} the same section lengthens
trajectories and reduces success, which is the behavioral component
and the one that hurts. Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization} prices the
accounting term; the behavioral term is what the anti-correlation of
Section~\ref{sec:disc-anticorr} describes.

Let $R_{\mathrm{base}}$ and $T_{\mathrm{base}}$ be the mean rounds and
mean input tokens per round of the memory-free agent on a task, and
let $\Delta R = R_{\mathrm{base}} - R_{\mathrm{mem}}$ be the round
reduction the memory produces. Injection adds a per-round overhead
$\Delta T = T_{\mathrm{mem}} - T_{\mathrm{base}}$. The memory
\emph{amortizes} (saves net input tokens) if and only if

\begin{equation}\label{eq:amortization}
\underbrace{\Delta R \cdot T_{\mathrm{base}}}_{\text{rounds no longer
paid}} \;>\; \underbrace{\Delta T \cdot
R_{\mathrm{mem}}}_{\text{overhead on every surviving round}} .
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization} makes the trade explicit: a memory
section that costs a few hundred tokens per round must buy a
substantial fraction of a round to break even. For the configurations
measured in this paper ($T_{\mathrm{base}} \approx 1{,}400$ tokens per
round, $R_{\mathrm{mem}} \approx 6$ rounds, $\Delta T \approx
200$--$270$ tokens), the break-even round reduction is $\Delta R^{*} =
\Delta T \cdot R_{\mathrm{mem}} / T_{\mathrm{base}} \approx 0.9$--$1.2$
rounds: the memory must eliminate about one full round of
interaction to pay for itself. The central empirical question is
whether realistic procedural memory clears that bar on the tasks where
it is actually needed. Our measurements
(Section~\ref{sec:results}) show that it clears it only on a task the
model already knows --- where the memory is redundant --- and misses
it by a factor of $3$--$6\times$ on fresh tasks --- where it would
matter. We refer to this as the \emph{anti-correlation} between when
memory helps and when it is needed.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
% Fuente: docs/project-memory-design.md, braze-memory (crate),
% docs/paper2-memory-distillation-protocol-2026-07-16.md (bench A→B→H),
% docs/hypothesis-2026-08-04-project-memory-ab.md (diseño A/B + gate).

\subsection{Harness, model, and benchmark
infrastructure}\label{sec:method-harness}

Both studies run on \braze{}, a local-first agentic coding harness
written in Rust whose design and evaluation methodology are described
in a companion study \citep{parra2026scaffolding}. The harness executes the
standard agentic loop: a system prompt carrying the tool inventory
(and, when enabled, a memory section), followed by \emph{rounds} in
which the model emits tool calls, the harness executes them in a
sandbox, and the tool results are appended to the conversation. The
property that drives Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization} is structural,
not incidental: on every round the harness re-sends the entire growing
conversation, including any injected memory section, so a
$c$-token memory costs $c \times R$ input tokens per task, not $c$.

The executor model in both studies is \texttt{gpt-oss:20b}, a
mixture-of-experts model (20B total, $\sim$3.6B active parameters)
running on a dedicated LAN inference node (16\,GB RAM); no other
workload shares the node during a sweep. Study~1 serves the model
through an Ollama server; Study~2 uses \braze{}'s in-process
\texttt{LocalBackend} (llama.cpp with the model's canonical Harmony
chat template and reference GGUF weights), with context window
32{,}768 tokens, output cap 12{,}288 tokens, temperature 0.2, and a
900\,s per-task timeout; the full environment tier is recorded in each
run's metadata. Each benchmark run receives a hermetic sandbox (a
fresh directory containing only the task's setup files) and is
graded by objective oracles; for compile-repair tasks the oracle runs
real \texttt{cargo check} on the resulting tree. The runner records
pass/fail, rounds, input and output tokens, wall time, and schema
validation failures per run.

\subsection{The two memory mechanisms under
test}\label{sec:method-mechanisms}

\paragraph{LearnedPlaybook (Study 1).}\sloppy A typed unit of
procedural memory defined by a JSON Schema: identity and lifecycle
fields, a procedural payload (\texttt{task\_family}, \texttt{applies\_when},
\texttt{preconditions}, \texttt{method\_steps}, \texttt{verification},
\texttt{avoid}, \texttt{escalate\_if}), and provenance
(\texttt{source}, \texttt{evidence}). The lifecycle is
\texttt{candidate} $\to$ \texttt{approved} $\to$ \texttt{validated}
$\to$ \texttt{trusted} (or \texttt{retired}); in the distillation
design, approval of a candidate requires only safety checks (schema
validity, no sensitive data, no concrete file names from the source
session, objective verification steps, an escalation clause); no
semantic hand-editing is allowed, so that tutor distillation is not conflated
with human authorship. The benchmark injects a playbook by rendering
it as a structured text section (title, family, applies-when, method,
verification, avoid, escalate-if) into the system prompt, truncated by
whole lines under a fixed 500-token budget. In Study~1 the playbook is
\emph{human-written} (the strongest condition a priori: an expert
author, no distillation noise), so the measured ceiling bounds every
automated variant from above.

\paragraph{ProjectMemory hook (Study 2).} The production form:
a harness hook that listens on the engine's event bus and records,
deterministically and with zero model calls, the paths of files
successfully written or edited into a small per-project JSON state; at
the start of a new session the harness renders a capped ``project
memory'' section (files touched in previous sessions) into the system
prompt. The capture is deliberately deterministic, with no model-written
summaries, because the same project had previously measured that
free-prose context degrades small executors. The lever is off by
default and enabled per-arm by the benchmark's ablation flags.

\subsection{Study 1: the multi-session benchmark
A$\to$B$\to$H and pilot M1}\label{sec:method-m1}

The benchmark models procedural transfer across sessions. In session
\textbf{A} the local model fails a task under an objective check and a
tutor intervention produces a playbook; \textbf{B} tasks are
same-family tasks with different modules, names, and constants, and
measure whether the playbook transfers; the holdout \textbf{H} is a
task outside the family and measures what an inapplicable playbook
costs. The pilot reported here (milestone M1) runs the benchmark
without a live tutor: the playbook is the human-written ceiling
described above, for the family \texttt{rust\_compile\_repair}
(borrow-checker repair in Rust).

The three B tasks were chosen to span the family: \textsc{original}
(the canonical E0502 borrow bug, which the model largely passes
unaided, i.e.\ saturated), \textsc{loop} (E0502 arising through
iteration), and \textsc{move} (E0382, use of moved value). The holdout
is a Rust task not involving borrow errors, run \emph{with} the
playbook attached. Arms are \texttt{none} vs.\
\texttt{human-playbook}, $n=20$ per cell with identical seeds
(42--61) and backend across arms: $7$ task-arms $\times\, 20 = 140$
runs. Following the pilot's own first finding, we report the three
quantities of Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization} together (round
reduction $\Delta R$, per-round overhead $\Delta T$, and net token
delta) so a reader can distinguish ``the playbook saved rounds''
from ``the playbook cost nothing'' from ``the playbook cost but the
turn was short anyway.'' Pass rates are compared with Fisher's exact
test and pass-rate differences carry Newcombe 95\% intervals; rounds
and wall time use two-sided Welch $t$-tests with Welch intervals,
Holm-corrected across the three task pairs within each endpoint
family. Table~\ref{tab:m1} reports point estimates; inference is in
the text.

Study~1 was subsequently replicated on a second executor:
\texttt{ornith:9b}, a dense 9B-parameter model (Q4\_K\_M) that
saturates the project's easier benchmark suite, served through the
same node with a 300\,s per-task timeout. The replication was
pre-registered before launch with the same design, seeds, arms, and
analysis plan, plus one rule specific to replication: the
saturated/fresh classification is re-derived from the memory-free
arms alone, before any playbook arm is read, so the prediction is
relative to the new executor's own knowledge rather than the
original's.

\subsection{Study 2: project-memory A/B with a same-prompt
control}\label{sec:method-pm}

Study 2 evaluates the ProjectMemory hook on \braze{}'s discriminating
suite: 34 editing tasks with a \texttt{cargo check} oracle, calibrated
near the executor's capability frontier ($\approx$2.9\,pp per item).
Three arms: \texttt{baseline} (hook off), \texttt{empty} (hook on;
since the benchmark opens a fresh session per repetition, the memory
is always empty and renders nothing: the same-prompt control), and
\texttt{seeded} (hook on; the benchmark synthesizes the memory a
previous session would have left, using the task's own setup files as
the touched-file list, with zero experimenter-authored content). Three
repetitions per arm at fixed seed and temperature 0.2 give $3 \times 3
\times 34 = 306$ runs. The primary contrast is pass rate under
McNemar's exact test on (task, repetition) pairs, Holm-corrected
across the two contrasts against baseline. Because repetitions of the
same task share prompt and oracle, pair-level McNemar overstates the
effective sample; we therefore also report task-level (cluster-aware)
tests: exact sign and Wilcoxon signed-rank over the 34 per-task
pass counts. Secondary metrics are input tokens, rounds, and wall
time.

The decision criteria were pre-registered before launch:
(1)~a \emph{plumbing gate}, evaluated first: if the empty arm
differs from baseline by more than 2 paired discordant cells, the
infrastructure is investigated before any other contrast is read;
(2)~a promotion signal (\texttt{seeded} $-$ \texttt{baseline} $\geq
+3$ tasks with $p<0.05$ \emph{and} \texttt{seeded} $\geq$
\texttt{empty}), which at most \emph{opens} a promotion discussion,
since the study measures only the single-session prompt-side effect,
not cross-session value; (3)~a symmetric harm criterion; (4)~the null
as the declared expectation (the registered prior: null or slightly
negative, since the touched-file list adds little the task prompt does
not already say, and costs tokens per round); and (5)~no iteration
allowed: the sweep is a measurement, not a tuning loop.

\subsection{Pre-registration and
reproducibility}\label{sec:method-prereg}

The project follows a git-only registration convention: each
hypothesis document --- research question, arms, metrics, decision
criteria, and an honest prior --- is committed to the project's public
repository before its sweep is launched, so that registration is
verifiable against the repository's public history rather than
self-asserted.

We audited our own compliance, and it is asymmetric. The order is
externally verifiable for \textbf{Study~2} (hypothesis committed
2026-08-04, first data 2026-08-06) and for the \textbf{replication of
Study~1} (hypothesis 2026-08-15, data 2026-08-16): any reader can
check both with a single command against the public history. It is
\emph{not} verifiable for the Study~1 pilot itself, whose protocol,
hypothesis, decision record and data all entered the public
repository in one commit after the pilot had run. Those documents
carry earlier internal dates and were written before the sweep, but
external precedence cannot be established from the record, and we do
not ask the reader to take it on trust. We therefore report the
pilot as \emph{exploratory} and rest this paper's confirmatory
claims --- the same-prompt control, the promotion criterion met in
letter and invalidated in premise, and the replication --- on the two
studies whose registration is verifiable.

The hypothesis documents are additionally archived at

the reliable findings of Study~1 are the efficiency quantities,
not the pass rates. What the data do establish is that the efficiency
gain on the saturated task is the only positive result of the entire
pilot, and it does not generalize even within the same bug family.

a paired memory-free arm, so its numbers are descriptive, not a
contrast: with the playbook attached to an out-of-family task the

does not derail the model, and its per-round tax simply has nothing to
amortize against there. For this executor the failure mode of
prompt-injected memory is economic, not behavioral --- a distinction
the replication of Section~\ref{sec:results-ornith} sharpens.

fall in playbook arms, so censoring at the 300\,s cap biases the
measured harm \emph{downward}.

locates the direction in which it moves with scale. On the 20B
executor the failure mode is economic: the playbook is followed but
not worth its tax. On the 9B executor it is also behavioral: the
playbook actively degrades success on exactly the tasks where memory
would be needed. The a priori plausible expectation that a weaker
model has more rounds of headroom for memory to save is measured here
and refuted: the weaker executor is hurt, not helped, consistent with
the companion study's finding that prose planning harms small
executors \citep{parra2026scaffolding}.

\subsection{Study 2: the same-prompt control removes the headline
effect}\label{sec:results-pm}

The second study evaluates the harness-integrated form of the same
idea: a project-memory hook that injects, into the system prompt, the
files touched in previous sessions. Because the benchmark opens a
fresh session per repetition, the plain hook arm runs with empty
memory and renders \emph{nothing}: its prompt is identical to the
baseline by construction. That accident of plumbing is
methodologically precious: the empty arm is a \textbf{same-prompt
control}, and any paired discordance between it and the baseline is an
in-sweep measurement of the noise floor. A third arm
(\emph{seeded}) synthesizes the memory a previous session would have
left, with zero experimenter-authored content. That content is
deliberately thin (a list of files the task prompts largely repeat,
as the pre-registered prior already noted), so this study bounds the
attributable effect of a realistic cheap injection; probing rich
procedural content is Study~1's role. The two studies carry different
evidential weight and we read them accordingly.

Read without the control arm, Table~\ref{tab:pm} is a publishable
positive: seeded beats baseline by $+13$ tasks with a Holm-corrected
$p=0.021$, meeting the pre-registered promotion criterion \emph{in its
letter}. The control arm, however, invalidates the premise: with a
byte-identical prompt, the empty arm rose $+9$ on its own, and the
only contrast that isolates the \emph{content} of the memory
(seeded vs.\ empty) is null ($+4$, $p=0.541$). Attributing the
headline to the memory would have been exactly the error the control
arm existed to prevent. The clustering structure independently makes
the same point: repetitions of a task are not independent pairs, and
at task level (34 per-task pass counts) the headline contrast weakens
to sign-test $p=0.065$ and Wilcoxon $p=0.054$: the $+13$ does not
survive cluster-aware analysis even before the control arm is
consulted, while seeded vs.\ empty stays null under every treatment
(task-level $p=0.58$--$0.59$). The lever was not promoted.

The pre-registered plumbing gate fired first: baseline vs.\ empty
showed 21 paired discordant cells against a pre-registered threshold
of $\leq 2$. The diagnostic (preserved sessions on the four most
discordant tasks) established that (i) round-1 prompts were
token-identical across arms in all 12 pairs --- proven, not assumed;
(ii) no trajectory ever observed the hook's on-disk state; and (iii)
a re-run inverted the direction of the discordances. Verdict: the 21
discordances were noise. The collateral finding is itself a result:
\textbf{the noise floor is configuration-specific}: the $\leq 2$
threshold, borrowed from the floor measured on an easier suite,
underestimated the discriminating-suite floor by an order of magnitude
($\sim$20\% of cells flip under identical prompts at temperature 0.2).
Every future A/B in this regime needs an in-sweep same-prompt control
rather than a floor imported from another configuration.

\subsection{Cost of the injected section}

% síntesis M1 (200-270 tokens/ronda del playbook).
The two studies bracket the injection-cost regime that
Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization} prices. The M1 playbook adds
$\sim$200--270 tokens \emph{per round} (a methodology section the
designer consults every round); the project-memory section is small
($\sim$40 tokens in round 1) but correspondingly carries no measurable
content signal. Between them: the injection cheap enough to be
harmless was too thin to matter, and the injection rich enough to
matter was too expensive to amortize on fresh tasks. Two caveats
bound this reading. The two points differ in content type as well as
size, and they come from different studies; and the middle of the
size axis is unmeasured: a short, task-tuned procedural hint of,
say, 50 tokens would face a break-even of only $\sim$0.2 rounds under
Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization}, and the variant that carries the
memory only for the first rounds of a turn and then drops it halves
the tax at fixed content. The frontier is narrow at its measured
points; the middle of the axis is open.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% Fuente: outline § contribución 4 + § threats + nota-lectura-autodesign
% + hypothesis-2026-08-13-sc-retention (forward reference).

\subsection{The anti-correlation is the finding with
teeth}\label{sec:disc-anticorr}

The single pattern that organizes both studies is that memory pays
where it is redundant and does not pay where it would be needed. The
mechanism is visible in the preserved sessions of Study~1: on the
fresh tasks the model \emph{follows} the playbook: it applies the
correct fix and is not confused by the advice; it simply does not
arrive faster for having read it. Round savings come from skipping
deliberation the model can already do on its own; they do not come
from making hard reasoning easier. Prompt-injected procedural memory
therefore behaves as a reminder, not a teacher, and a reminder is
worth the most on exactly the tasks where it is worth nothing. This is
not a quirk of one playbook: the same asymmetry reappears in Study~2,
where the only contrast that isolates the memory's content is null,
and in the holdout, where the inapplicable playbook costs its fixed
tax without behavioral harm. The failure mode is economic:
Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization} with $\Delta R$ collapsing precisely
when the task is fresh --- until the executor weakens. On the 9B
replication the same playbook turns from unprofitable to harmful:
$\Delta R$ goes negative and success drops significantly on fresh
tasks. A reminder the 20B model can afford to carry becomes a
distraction the 9B model cannot. The anti-correlation survives the
change of scale; what changes is the price of ignoring it.

\subsection{Why the null is worth publishing}\label{sec:disc-null}

``Distill the cloud tutor's intervention into a reusable playbook and
amortize the escalation'' is exactly the kind of technique that sounds
obviously good: plausible mechanism, appealing economics, easy to
adopt without measurement. Our original protocol for this line
proposed it as a positive contribution; the data refused. The project
this work belongs to has accumulated a discipline of pre-registered
null results at the harness level: a prose planner that actively
harmed small executors, tool deferral that proved cheaper but not
free, constrained decoding that failed to beat the harness's own
repair ladder \citep{parra2026scaffolding}. The present null is the highest
conceptual stake among them because the technique is the most
attractive, and because \emph{both} an efficiency framing (Study~1)
and an effectiveness framing (Study~2) fail independently, by
different mechanisms, under different measurement designs. What
survives is quantitative and reusable: the bar a memory must clear
($\Delta R^{*} \approx 0.9$--$1.2$ rounds in this configuration) and
the measured shortfall ($3$--$6\times$) on fresh tasks.

\subsection{The same-prompt control as a transferable
instrument}\label{sec:disc-control}

Without the empty arm, Study~2 is a publishable false positive: $+13$
tasks, McNemar $p=0.011$, Holm-corrected, meeting a pre-registered
promotion criterion. Every element of conventional rigor was present
--- and insufficient, because the criterion's premise (that a paired
significant difference against baseline reflects the treatment)
assumed a noise floor imported from an easier suite. The measured
floor on the discriminating suite was an order of magnitude higher:
$\sim$20\% of paired cells flip under byte-identical prompts at
temperature 0.2. The general lesson for agentic-system evaluation in
the small-model regime: when the suite is deliberately calibrated near
the model's capability frontier, per-configuration noise dominates,
and the only floor that can be trusted is one measured \emph{inside
the same sweep}: an arm whose prompt is identical to baseline by
construction. The instrument is cheap (one extra arm), demands no new
tooling, and converts ``significant against baseline'' into the only
question that matters: significant against \emph{what}. The measured
floor also yields the suite's minimum detectable effect: with
$\sim$21 noise discordant pairs, an exact McNemar at $\alpha=0.05$
requires a net asymmetry of at least 11 pairs ($\approx$11\,pp on
this suite); at three repetitions, any true effect smaller than
that is invisible regardless of its reality. Smaller expected effects
need proportionally more repetitions, or a sequential (anytime-valid)
design in place of fixed-$n$ testing.

\subsection{Two measured exits from the wrong side of the
frontier}\label{sec:disc-directions}

Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization} has exactly two exits, and the data
locate both. \emph{Lower $\Delta T$}: move the memory out of the
per-round prompt and retrieve it on demand (a tool call or a
just-in-time context file), so its cost is paid once per consultation
rather than $R$ times per task. The condition then compares one
retrieval against rounds saved, a far weaker bar than the
$\sim$1{,}200 tokens per task the M1 playbook pays before helping.
\emph{Raise $\Delta R$}: target interventions at task classes the
model fails outright, where success converts a full budget of burned
rounds into a short trajectory and $\Delta R$ is large by
construction. The replication tempers this exit: on the 9B executor
injected guidance reduced success outright, so a class-unlocking
intervention must clear a behavioral bar, not only an economic one.
The M1 playbook did the opposite: it encoded a
methodology for a family the model already navigates, which is why
its best case was worth only one round. To be precise about what is
measured: this paper quantifies the bar these designs must clear (the cost
structure of Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization} with the numbers
above), not the designs themselves, which remain future work with a
priced target. A third boundary is
scoped out deliberately: session-scoped constraints (user rules that
must survive context compaction) are the limit case of durable memory
whose value is \emph{compliance} rather than round savings.
Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization} does not price them, and a separate
pre-registered experiment in the same project addresses them. It has
since closed without adopting the lever: on the 9B executor a
durable-constraint route solved $5/40$ constraint tasks against
$0/40$ for the control---every discordant pair favoring the route, at
$\sim$5\% \emph{lower} token cost---but $p = 0.0625$ at that sample
size, and on the 20B executor both arms floored at $0/40$, leaving
the lever unmeasurable there rather than absent. Neither outcome is
something our condition can price: compliance value arrived at
negative context cost, and a two-armed floor reports on the benchmark
rather than on the treatment.

\subsection{Relation to compaction loss and meta-harness
optimization}\label{sec:disc-related}

Two recent lines make the context-budget economics of agent memory
urgent. Work on compaction loss \citep{wang2026compint} shows that the
harness silently \emph{loses} memory: session-scoped constraints
survive context compaction only a minority of the time, and the
authors argue for architectural separation of durable state. Our
result is the complementary half of the same budget: even when memory
is durably preserved and injected, the injection itself carries a
per-round price that fresh tasks do not repay. A principled memory
subsystem has to price both sides (what to preserve across
compaction and what to re-inject per round), and
Equation~\eqref{eq:amortization} is the acceptance criterion for the
injection side.

Meta-harness optimization \citep{luo2026autodesign} formalizes the
harness as an optimization target with memory as one of its
components, accepting a proposed harness update when a train score
improves and a dev score does not regress: point estimates, no
statistics. Study~2 is a concrete demonstration of what that
acceptance gate would do in this regime: it would have accepted a
$+13$-task improvement that a same-prompt control shows to be
unattributable to the treatment. In the small-model regime, where
suites sit near the capability frontier and the in-sweep noise floor
reaches $\sim$20\% of cells, an automated harness optimizer needs the
machinery used here (pre-registered criteria, paired exact tests
with correction, and a same-prompt control arm) as its acceptance
gate, or its optimization record fills with noise promoted to design.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}
% Fuente: outline § threats.

\paragraph{External validity.} Study 1 uses one task family
(\texttt{rust\_compile\_repair}); its result is measured on two
executors of different architecture (a 20B mixture-of-experts and a
dense 9B) on one hardware and serving tier, while Study 2 uses the
20B alone. The frontier remains configuration-dependent: a different
task family might admit interventions that unlock whole classes of
failure rather than shave steps off a known procedure, which would
move the break-even point. The once-plausible expectation that a
weaker model has more rounds of headroom for memory to save is no
longer a speculation we owe the reader: the replication measured it
and found the opposite (Section~\ref{sec:results-ornith}). We claim
the \emph{condition} and the measurement procedure as general, and
the measured \emph{positions} of the frontier as specific to this
task family and these two executors.

\paragraph{Construct validity.} The null concerns memory \emph{in the
prompt}. Retrieval-on-demand memory has a different cost profile by
construction and is untested here; we present it as the measured
design direction (Section~\ref{sec:disc-directions}), not as a
finding. The original protocol's remaining conditions
(\texttt{summary}, distilled \texttt{procedural},
\texttt{lead-fallback}) were not piloted: every one of them adds at
least the injection cost of the human-written playbook that already
failed to amortize, so their expected outcome is recorded as a
declared prediction rather than confirmed by further measurement, a
scope decision we make explicit rather than hide.

\paragraph{Internal validity and reliability.} The serving stack is
not bit-exact under fixed seeds: in Study 1, a wall-time effect that
was significant in one sweep ($p=0.008$) did not replicate in an
independent re-run, while the primary token effect replicated in
direction and magnitude; no conclusion in this paper rests on a
secondary wall-time contrast. In Study 2, our own pre-registered
plumbing gate was miscalibrated by an order of magnitude (its
$\leq 2$-cell threshold was imported from the noise floor of an easier
suite), and we report that miscalibration as a finding
(Section~\ref{sec:results-pm}) rather than repair it post hoc. All
headline contrasts are paired, exact, and multiplicity-corrected; the
decisive contrast is read against an in-sweep same-prompt control
rather than an imported floor; and both pre-registrations forbade
iteration on the treatment, which was honored.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Distilling a cloud tutor's rescue into reusable procedural memory is
the kind of technique that sounds too obviously good to bother
measuring. Measured under pre-registration, objective oracles, and a
same-prompt control, it does not pay where it is needed: injected
memory amortizes --- at best marginally --- only on tasks the model
already knows, misses break-even by $3$--$6\times$ on fresh ones, and its apparently
significant effectiveness gain dissolves against a control arm with a
byte-identical prompt. The amortization condition of
Section~\ref{sec:model} states exactly what would have to change for
the technique to pay: cut the per-round injection cost by retrieving
memory on demand instead of carrying it, or raise the rounds saved by
aiming interventions at task classes the model fails outright. Those
two exits, each with a bar this paper has measured, are where this
line of work continues.

The methodological export stands on its own. In the small-model
regime, where discriminating suites sit near the capability frontier
and noise floors are configuration-specific, an A/B comparison without
an in-sweep same-prompt control is not interpretable: ours would have
promoted a false positive that met every conventional bar, including
its own pre-registered criterion. Any system that optimizes harness
memory automatically (and the meta-harness line makes that agenda
explicit) will need this machinery as its acceptance gate.
