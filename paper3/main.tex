\begin{abstract}
Systems that optimize an agent's harness while keeping the model fixed must
decide, at every step, whether a candidate beats the incumbent. Recent gates
make that decision with a statistical test, and the most careful of them
calibrate their threshold against noise measured on repeated runs of the base
harness, then keep it fixed. We ask whether a single calibration is enough. Using
the archive of a single agentic coding harness (140 sweeps, 32 backend
specifications, mostly small local models, 14{,}790 runs in groups that repeat an
identical configuration), we treat every pair of runs of the same configuration
as a null comparison, so that every change a gate accepts on it is a false
acceptance. We find that noise is a property of the configuration, not of the
suite: within one sweep, the share of tasks that flip between identical runs
differs across configurations by a median of 11.1 percentage points, and one
incumbent in three is noisier than its base. A tolerance calibrated once on the
base accepts 6.9\% of null changes (17.8\% when the base is the quietest
configuration); re-deriving it from each configuration's own repetitions lowers
this to 0.8\% and matches or raises power at every effect size we inject, because
a fixed tolerance is too narrow for noisier incumbents and too wide for quieter
ones. The decision rules of published optimizers accept between 1.1\% and 26.3\%
of null changes, and the margin alone moves a rule by more than an order of
magnitude. We recommend that gates re-derive their threshold per incumbent with a
tail estimator and report the false-acceptance rate of the threshold they use.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Statistical gates have made the accept step of harness optimization a test, and
the strongest of them calibrate the test against measured noise. In our archive
that calibration is not a property of the benchmark: the noise of identical runs
changes with the configuration, so a threshold measured on the base is
miscalibrated for the incumbents it will judge, accepting null changes from
noisier ones and rejecting real improvements from quieter ones. Re-deriving the
threshold for each incumbent, from its own repetitions and with a tail
estimator, removes most false acceptances without costing power; its price is
repeated runs, which an evolution can accumulate over rounds. A gate should
report its noise estimate, the threshold it produced, and the false-acceptance
rate of that threshold on its own null comparisons.
